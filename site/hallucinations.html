The model, as a circuit

Allen's model is a claim about signal flow. Hearing is not what breaks — someone who hallucinates hears the world exactly as anyone else does. What breaks is the handling of the brain's own inner speech: the control regions normally send a copy of the command with it, marking it as self-generated, and when that copy fails to arrive the same inner speech is heard as a voice from somewhere else.

Hearing the world unchanged in both

Sound arrives from the world, is relayed by the thalamus and is worked up through the auditory cortices until it is heard. This pathway is the same either way: hallucination is not a failure of hearing, and someone who hallucinates hears the world as anyone else does.

The brain's own inner speech tagged as one's own

Not only in illness

Boksa opens by unsettling the assumption that hallucinations belong to schizophrenia alone. She adds one crucial caveat: it is unknown at present whether hallucinations are generated by similar mechanisms in patients and in healthy people.

What the imaging found

Allen and colleagues reviewed structural, functional and connectivity studies of the hallucinating brain. Boksa's summary of their findings, and of the model they propose.

MethodFinding
StructuralThe most consistent result is reduced grey matter volume in the superior temporal gyrus, including primary auditory cortex. One fairly large study also found volume reduction in the DLPFC, suggesting faulty frontotemporal interactions may underlie the sense that hallucinations are involuntary.
FunctionalStudies of actively hallucinating participants generally report increased activity in language areas and primary auditory cortex, strongly implicating superior and middle temporal gyri — though other nonsensory cortical and subcortical areas have been implicated too.
ConnectivityDiffusion tensor and functional imaging concur in showing altered connectivity among temporal, prefrontal and anterior cingulate regions.
Allen's modelOveractivity in primary and/or secondary auditory cortices, altered connectivity with inferior frontal language areas, and weakened control by anterior cingulate, prefrontal, premotor and cerebellar cortices.
LesionsIn psychologically normal individuals hallucinating after a lesion, the lesion was generally in the sensory pathway of the same modality as the hallucination.
TreatmentSlow rTMS over right temporoparietal cortex reduced treatment-resistant auditory hallucinations, mean effect size 0.76. Boksa offers a comparison: clozapine versus typical antipsychotics scored 0.48 on the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale — though that meta-analysis did not target hallucinations specifically.

Windows onto the mechanism

Three approaches Boksa reports, each with the reservation she attaches to it.

ApproachWhat it showedBoksa's reservation
Self-tickling A self-generated stimulus is attenuated because we anticipate its sensory consequences. Healthy controls found self-tickling less intense than an experimenter's touch; participants with auditory hallucinations or passivity phenomena did not discriminate between the two. The underlying mechanisms for such misattribution are not yet understood.
Hypnosis The right anterior cingulate activated in hypnotisable people both when hearing real speech and when instructed to hallucinate, but not when imagining speech — suggesting a role in attributing speech to an external source. Controls should also have activated the ACC on hearing real speech, and did not. Altered ACC activation also appears in non-hallucinatory hypnotic suggestion, so it may reflect suggestibility itself.
REM sleep Hallucinations and delusions are regular features of REM. On waking from REM dreams, women — but not men — showed a deficit in monitoring self-generated versus externally generated stimuli. Offered as a potential insight rather than an established parallel.

Where it stops

The editorial's closing argument, which is as much about the limits of the evidence as about the evidence.

In studies with human participants, neural processes can only be shown to correlate with hallucinations, not to definitively cause them. Every finding above is an association.

Animal models, which are normally where causal evidence comes from, do not resolve it. Monkeys and dogs can apparently tell their own vocalisations from another animal's, so one might expect them to misattribute under abnormal conditions — but assessing whether an animal is hallucinating is problematic. Monkeys given drugs known to produce psychosis in humans do show behavioural changes, yet what has been categorised as hallucinatory-like behaviour, such as responding to nonapparent stimuli or staring for extended periods, is too nonspecific for research into mechanism.

Boksa's own conclusion is that although useful insight has been gained, we still have a long way to go to fully understand what causes the voices and visions of schizophrenia.
