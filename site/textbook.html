Making the nervous system visible

01Foundation

Seeing a single cell

By the end of this section you should be able to

  • Explain why the Golgi method's low impregnation rate was essential to its success.
  • State the neuron doctrine and contrast it with reticular theory.
  • Describe the principal classes of glial cell and their functional roles.
  • Justify why cellular resolution was a precondition for circuit-level explanation.

Nervous tissue is nearly transparent, densely packed, and interdigitated at a scale below the resolving limit of nineteenth-century light microscopy. Before 1873, a section of cortex under the microscope was a grey feltwork in which no boundary between one cell and the next could be established. The central methodological problem of early neuroscience was not magnification. It was contrast.

Camillo Golgi's reazione nera — the black reaction — solved it by accident of chemistry rather than design. Tissue hardened in potassium dichromate and then immersed in silver nitrate precipitates silver chromate inside a small and apparently arbitrary subset of cells, filling the soma and its processes completely and opaquely while leaving surrounding tissue unstained. The stained cell stands in silhouette against an empty field. Its dendrites can be followed to their tips; its axon can be traced away from the soma.

The critical property is the one that looks like a defect. The method impregnates only a small percentage of the cells in any given section, and no reliable means of controlling which cells has ever been established. Had it stained everything, it would have revealed nothing — as Figure 1.1 demonstrates, a fully impregnated field is as uninformative as an unstained one. Sparseness is the resolution.

Two readings of the same picture

Golgi read his own images as support for reticular theory: the proposition that the processes of nerve cells fuse into a continuous protoplasmic network, a syncytium through which excitation diffuses. Santiago Ramón y Cajal, applying the same stain to young and embryonic tissue where processes are shorter and less entangled, read them as support for the opposite view — the neuron doctrine, which holds that the nervous system is composed of discrete cells that contact one another without cytoplasmic continuity.

The disagreement is the clearest available illustration of a principle that recurs throughout this chapter: a method delivers images, not conclusions. The two men shared the 1906 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for work on the structure of the nervous system, and used their lectures to restate incompatible interpretations of it. Definitive resolution in Cajal's favour required electron microscopy in the 1950s, which visualised the synaptic cleft directly.

The cells that are not neurons

Cajal's contemporaries were already aware that neurons are a minority tissue. Glial cells — astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia in the central nervous system, and Schwann cells in the periphery — are present in numbers comparable to or exceeding neurons, and were long treated as passive scaffolding, a reading encoded in the name (glia, glue).

That reading is now untenable. Astrocytes regulate extracellular ion and neurotransmitter concentrations, contribute to the blood–brain barrier, and participate directly in synaptic signalling; oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells produce the myelin that determines axonal conduction velocity; microglia are the resident immune population and actively prune synapses during development. A method that labels only neurons — and the Golgi stain largely does — therefore returns a systematically partial picture of the tissue.

Retrieval practice

1. A colleague proposes improving the Golgi method by modifying the chemistry so that it impregnates 100% of neurons. Explain, in terms of the resolving problem the method was designed to solve, why this would destroy its usefulness.

Show answer
The Golgi method does not increase optical resolution; it increases contrast between one cell and its neighbours. Tracing a dendrite or axon to its termination requires that the process be visually separable from all other processes in the same volume. Because neuropil is densely interdigitated, a stain that filled every neuron would restore the original undifferentiated feltwork at higher optical density — every process visible, none traceable. Sparse, stochastic labelling is what converts an opaque volume into a set of individually resolvable objects. The same logic underlies modern sparse-labelling strategies such as single-cell dye fills and stochastic multicolour reporters.

2. Golgi and Cajal examined material prepared by the same technique and reached opposite structural conclusions. What does this imply about the relationship between data and interpretation in method-driven science?

Show answer
That instruments constrain but do not determine conclusions. The Golgi images were consistent with both continuity and contiguity because the technique's resolution could not distinguish a fused process from two apposed processes below the diffraction limit. The dispute was only resolvable by a method with sufficient resolution to image the cleft itself — electron microscopy. Generalisation: when two interpretations are both compatible with the available data, the productive move is to identify the measurement that would separate them, rather than to argue from the existing images.
02Access

Writing into the genome

By the end of this section you should be able to

  • Explain how promoter specificity converts a genetic tool into an anatomical one.
  • Describe site-specific recombination and its use in conditional gene expression.
  • Predict which cells will be labelled by a given driver–reporter combination.
  • Identify the principal failure modes of genetic targeting.

Golgi's sparseness was stochastic — the experimenter could not choose which cells to see. The defining methodological advance of the past four decades has been the replacement of chance with address: labelling, recording from, and manipulating cells selected by their molecular identity rather than by luck or by position under a pipette.

The logic is uniform across techniques. A gene of interest — a fluorescent protein, a calcium indicator, a light-sensitive ion channel — is placed under the control of regulatory sequence that is active only in a defined population. Cells that transcribe that promoter make the protein. Cells that do not, do not. Anatomical specificity is inherited from gene expression.

In practice, few promoters are specific enough on their own, and the standard solution is intersectional targeting using site-specific recombinases. A driver line expresses Cre recombinase under a cell-type-restricted promoter. A separate reporter allele carries the effector gene interrupted by a transcriptional stop cassette flanked by loxP sites. Only in cells expressing both is the stop excised and the effector transcribed. Adding a second, independent recombinase — commonly Flp with its FRT sites — permits targeting the intersection of two expression patterns, which is frequently far more specific than either alone.

Where genetic targeting fails

Three failure modes recur and should be assumed present until excluded. Developmental expression: recombination is permanent, so a promoter transiently active in a progenitor labels all its descendants, regardless of adult identity. Leak: incomplete stop cassettes and low-level promoter activity produce labelling outside the intended population. Ectopic expression: the transgene's genomic insertion site and the truncation of regulatory sequence in a construct both cause expression patterns that diverge from the endogenous gene. In consequence, a driver line is an empirical object requiring characterisation in its own right, not a specification that can be trusted from its name.

Retrieval practice

1. A Cre line named for a gene expressed in adult inhibitory interneurons labels a scattered population of excitatory cortical neurons as well. Give the most likely explanation and one experiment that would test it.

Show answer
Most likely transient developmental expression: the promoter was active in a progenitor or immature cell that later adopted an excitatory identity, and because Cre-mediated excision is irreversible the label persists although the gene is now silent. Test by comparing the Cre-reporter pattern against direct detection of the endogenous transcript in adult tissue (in situ hybridisation or single-cell RNA sequencing). Concordance argues for ongoing expression; discordance argues for a developmental lineage artefact. An inducible driver (CreER with tamoxifen given in adulthood) restricts recombination to a defined time window and separates the two possibilities directly.

2. Why does adding a second recombinase increase specificity more effectively than searching for a better single promoter?

Show answer
Because cell types are frequently defined by combinations of genes rather than by any single marker gene. If population A is defined by co-expression of genes X and Y, no single-promoter line can isolate it — the X promoter captures A plus everything else expressing X, and likewise for Y. An intersectional design computes the logical AND, so the labelled set is the overlap. The gain is combinatorial: two moderately selective promoters can yield an intersection narrower than either, and the approach scales without requiring the discovery of a perfectly unique marker.
03Taxonomy

How many kinds of cell?

By the end of this section you should be able to

  • Explain how single-cell transcriptomics generates a cell-type taxonomy.
  • Interpret a hierarchical classification of class, subclass, supertype and cluster.
  • State why a transcriptomic type is a hypothesis rather than a discovered kind.
  • Evaluate the case for a common nomenclature across laboratories.

If explanation in neuroscience proceeds by identifying the parts of a circuit and what each contributes, the parts list must exist first. For most of the twentieth century that list was compiled from morphology, firing pattern and a handful of immunohistochemical markers, and different laboratories produced incompatible catalogues. Single-cell RNA sequencing changed the problem's character by supplying a high-dimensional, quantitative and reproducible measurement of every cell's transcriptional state.

The procedure is now standard: dissociate tissue into single cells or nuclei, capture and barcode each cell's messenger RNA, sequence, and assign every cell a vector of gene-expression counts. Cells are then embedded and clustered, and clusters are proposed as types. Applied across cortical areas, this approach resolved populations that had been indistinguishable by earlier criteria, and demonstrated that some types are shared between areas while others are area-specific.

Scaled to a whole brain, the resulting taxonomy is hierarchical. The Allen Brain Cell Atlas organises roughly four million profiled mouse cells into a nested classification of 34 classes, 338 subclasses, 1,201 supertypes and 5,322 clusters. The number that matters pedagogically is not 5,322 but the depth of the hierarchy: cell identity is graded and nested, not a flat list of kinds.

A type is a hypothesis

A cluster is a decision about where to cut a continuous distribution, and that decision depends on the clustering algorithm, its resolution parameter, the number of cells sampled, the dissociation protocol, and whether whole cells or nuclei were profiled. Increasing sequencing depth generally increases the number of recoverable clusters. There is no measurement internal to the transcriptomic data that fixes the correct number of types.

The consequence is that a transcriptomic type is a hypothesis about a biologically meaningful population, to be corroborated by evidence of a different kind: does the cluster occupy a distinct anatomical position, project to a distinct target, fire distinctively, develop from a distinct lineage? Types that survive this cross-modal test are on much firmer ground than types defined by expression alone. The corresponding community response was a proposal for shared classification criteria and a common nomenclature, so that populations identified in different laboratories can be recognised as the same population.

Retrieval practice

1. Two laboratories sequence the same cortical region and report 48 and 79 excitatory types respectively. Before concluding that either is wrong, what four methodological factors should you check?

Show answer
(i) Number of cells profiled and sequencing depth — more cells and deeper coverage resolve more clusters. (ii) Clustering algorithm and its resolution parameter, which directly sets granularity. (iii) Whole cells versus nuclei, and the dissociation protocol — these bias which transcripts and which cell classes survive, and dissociation stress itself alters expression. (iv) Anatomical boundaries of the dissection, since area-specific types will appear or vanish with the sampled territory. Only after these are matched does a genuine biological disagreement remain.

2. Why is cross-modal corroboration — anatomy, projection target, physiology, development — treated as stronger evidence for a type than transcriptomic separation alone?

Show answer
Because transcriptomic clustering can only report structure in the expression data, and that structure is partly an artefact of analytical choices. Independent modalities are not subject to the same artefacts, so agreement between them is unlikely to arise from a shared methodological bias. A cluster that also occupies a distinct layer, projects to a distinct target and has a distinct firing pattern is being detected by four largely independent measurements, which makes a common underlying biological cause far more probable than a clustering artefact.
04Strategy

Choosing an animal

By the end of this section you should be able to

  • Justify the choice of a model organism in terms of tractability and generality.
  • Compare nervous systems across four orders of magnitude in cell number.
  • Explain what makes a nervous system with an invariant cell lineage analytically valuable.
  • State the conditions under which a finding in one species licenses inference about another.

No laboratory studies the nervous system in general. Every result is obtained in a particular animal, and the choice of animal is a methodological decision with consequences as substantial as the choice of microscope. The trade-off is explicit: tractability decreases and relevance to human neuroscience increases as nervous systems get larger.

At one extreme is Caenorhabditis elegans. The hermaphrodite has exactly 302 neurons; the number and identity of those neurons are invariant between individuals; the developmental lineage of every cell is known; and the animal is transparent, so any cell can be imaged in the intact behaving organism. These properties permitted the reconstruction of the entire nervous system by serial-section electron microscopy — the first complete connectome of any animal. The reconstruction remained the only one of its kind for roughly three decades, and its assumptions and limitations have been re-examined as comparable datasets have become available.

Drosophila melanogaster occupies the middle of the range, with a brain of order 105 neurons: large enough to support learning, navigation, courtship and sleep, small enough to reconstruct completely. The adult female brain has now been mapped in full, yielding a wiring diagram of 139,255 proofread neurons together with their synaptic connections, with an accompanying whole-brain annotation assigning those neurons to cell types across multiple connectomes.

Mouse cortex is beyond complete reconstruction at present, but a cubic millimetre is not: a volume of mouse visual cortex has been reconstructed at synaptic resolution and combined with functional recordings of the same neurons, yielding roughly 75,000 neurons and about 500 million synapses in a single dataset. The human brain contains on the order of 86 billion neurons, some six orders of magnitude above C. elegans, and is not a candidate for exhaustive reconstruction by any current technique.

What transfers between species

Model organisms are used on the assumption that mechanisms are conserved even where anatomy is not. The assumption holds well at the molecular and cellular level — ion channels, synaptic release machinery and second-messenger cascades are broadly shared — and progressively less well as the level of description rises. A conclusion about voltage-gated channel gating in squid axon generalises confidently. A conclusion about the circuit implementation of a behaviour in fly generalises only as a hypothesis. The discipline required is to state, for each result, which level the claim is being made at.

Retrieval practice

1. Why does an invariant cell lineage make C. elegans exceptionally useful, beyond the small number of neurons?

Show answer
Invariance makes individual cells identifiable across animals. Neuron AVAL in one worm is the same neuron as AVAL in another, with the same lineage, position and connectivity. This means results from different animals can be pooled at single-cell resolution; a lesion, recording or manipulation can be targeted to a named cell reproducibly; and a single connectome is a statement about the species, not about one individual. In animals with variable cell number and position, none of these hold, and single-cell claims must be re-established statistically in each preparation.

2. A circuit mechanism for visual course control is established in Drosophila. State the strongest claim you can make about humans on that basis, and the evidence needed to strengthen it.

Show answer
The strongest legitimate claim is existence and plausibility: that this is a computationally sufficient solution to the problem, implementable in neural hardware, and therefore a candidate hypothesis for how other nervous systems solve it. It is not evidence that humans use it. Strengthening requires demonstrating homology of the participating cell types or genes, or independently testing the mechanism in a mammalian system — anatomical evidence of an analogous circuit motif, physiological evidence of the same computation, and ideally a causal manipulation reproducing the predicted behavioural effect.
05Structure

Wiring diagrams

By the end of this section you should be able to

  • Describe how a volumetric connectome is produced and what limits its scale.
  • Read a connectivity diagram organised by processing stage.
  • State what a connectome does and does not determine about function.
  • Distinguish structural connectivity from functional and effective connectivity.

A connectome is a map of which neurons contact which, ideally at the resolution of the individual synapse. Producing one at that resolution requires electron microscopy, because chemical synapses are identified by features — vesicle clusters, membrane apposition, a cleft of a few tens of nanometres — that are invisible to light.

The pipeline has been the same in outline since the 1970s and differs now only in throughput. Tissue is fixed, stained with heavy metals, embedded, and sectioned at thicknesses below 50 nm. Every section is imaged. The images are aligned into a volume, each neuronal process is segmented and traced between sections, and synapses are detected and assigned to pre- and postsynaptic partners. In the original C. elegans reconstruction this was performed by hand over more than a decade; in current work, segmentation is performed by convolutional networks with human proofreading, which is what made brain-scale reconstruction in the fly and millimetre-scale reconstruction in mouse cortex feasible.

What a wiring diagram cannot tell you

A connectome is a necessary constraint on circuit function and an insufficient explanation of it. It is a static anatomical description, and at least four functionally decisive properties are absent from it. Sign: whether a synapse is excitatory or inhibitory depends on transmitter and receptor, not on morphology. Weight: synaptic strength varies over orders of magnitude and changes with activity. Neuromodulation: transmitters released extrasynaptically reconfigure circuit dynamics without appearing as connections. State: the same anatomy supports different functional regimes depending on arousal, behavioural context and history.

This motivates a standard three-way distinction. Structural connectivity is anatomical contact. Functional connectivity is statistical dependence between activity in two regions or cells, which may be produced by a common input rather than by any direct link. Effective connectivity is the causal influence of one element on another, and is established only by perturbation. Conflating these is among the most common errors in the interpretation of network data, and it recurs at every scale from synapses to whole-brain imaging.

Retrieval practice

1. Two regions show strongly correlated activity but no direct anatomical connection. Give two explanations consistent with both observations.

Show answer
(i) Common input: a third region projects to both, imposing shared temporal structure without any direct link between them. (ii) Indirect (polysynaptic) coupling: the regions communicate via one or more intermediate relays, so influence is real but the anatomical path is not direct. A third possibility worth noting is a shared non-neural driver — for example a global physiological signal such as respiration or vascular fluctuation contaminating both measurements. Distinguishing these requires perturbation: silencing the candidate common input should abolish the correlation, whereas silencing an intermediate relay should abolish it only in the polysynaptic case.

2. Why does knowing every synapse in a circuit still leave its output underdetermined?

Show answer
Because anatomy specifies the graph but not the dynamics computed on it. The same graph produces different outputs depending on the sign of each connection, the weight of each connection, the intrinsic excitability of each neuron, and the neuromodulatory state, none of which are recoverable from structural images. Circuits with identical connectivity can be switched between qualitatively different regimes — for example between silence, tonic firing and rhythmic bursting — by modulators alone. A connectome therefore constrains the space of possible functions without selecting one from it.
06Function

Watching circuits work

By the end of this section you should be able to

  • Position the principal functional methods on axes of spatial and temporal resolution.
  • Explain the trade-off between resolution, coverage and invasiveness.
  • Describe how microbial opsins permit millisecond causal control of defined neurons.
  • Distinguish correlational from causal evidence about circuit function.

Structure constrains function; it does not reveal it. Determining what a circuit does requires measuring activity — and, if a causal claim is intended, changing it. No single method measures activity at all relevant scales, and the choice of method is a choice about which scale a question will be answered at.

The classical measurement is electrical. An intracellular or patch electrode reports membrane potential in one cell with sub-millisecond precision and is the only technique that resolves individual synaptic events; extracellular arrays sacrifice subthreshold detail for simultaneous recording from many cells. Optical measurement inverts the trade-off: genetically encoded calcium indicators report activity in thousands of identified cells at once, but calcium is a slow and indirect proxy for spiking, so temporal precision is degraded to the order of tens to hundreds of milliseconds.

From correlation to cause

Recording establishes that activity in a population covaries with a stimulus or behaviour. It cannot establish that the population produces the behaviour, because the same correlation is generated by a population that merely receives a copy of the relevant signal. Causal claims require perturbation, and the value of optogenetics is that it supplies perturbation at the timescale on which neurons actually operate.

The enabling discovery was that channelrhodopsin-2, an algal protein, is a directly light-gated cation channel that depolarises a membrane when illuminated with blue light — no additional cofactors, and gating fast enough to follow a light pulse. Expressing it in neurons under the genetic control described in Section 2 makes it possible to drive spiking in a molecularly defined population with millisecond precision. Combining a genetic address with an optical trigger converts the question does this population correlate with the behaviour? into is this population sufficient to produce it?

Sufficiency is still not necessity. Driving a population and observing the behaviour shows that the circuit can produce it, not that it normally does; the complementary experiment silences the population — optically, chemogenetically, or by lesion — and asks whether the behaviour survives. Both directions are vulnerable to compensation and to the recruitment of downstream circuitry that is not normally engaged, so a strong causal claim rests on convergent evidence from recording, activation and silencing rather than on any single manipulation.

Retrieval practice

1. Optogenetic activation of population P reliably evokes behaviour B. State precisely what has and has not been demonstrated.

Show answer
Demonstrated: activity in P is sufficient to evoke B under the conditions tested. Not demonstrated: that P is necessary for B; that P is engaged during natural production of B; or that the artificially imposed pattern resembles the endogenous one. Synchronous, high-intensity activation of an entire genetically defined population is physiologically unusual and may recruit downstream targets that natural, sparser, temporally structured activity would not. The necessity claim requires a loss-of-function experiment, and the naturalness claim requires recording during unperturbed behaviour.

2. Why can calcium imaging report from thousands of neurons where patch-clamp recording cannot, and what is surrendered?

Show answer
Because the measurement is optical and parallel: a single camera or scanned laser samples an entire field of view, so the number of simultaneously monitored cells is limited by optics rather than by the number of physical electrodes, and cells are identified by genetic label rather than by blind targeting. What is surrendered is temporal fidelity and subthreshold access. Calcium is an indirect proxy whose rise and decay kinetics are slower than the spikes producing it, so individual spikes in a high-frequency burst are not resolved, and synaptic potentials that do not trigger spikes are largely invisible.
07Behaviour

Measuring behaviour

By the end of this section you should be able to

  • Explain why behavioural measurement is the limiting factor in circuit neuroscience.
  • Describe markerless pose estimation and what it produces.
  • Distinguish a behavioural category imposed by an observer from one recovered from data.
  • Identify sources of bias in manually scored behavioural data.

Every claim that a circuit underlies a behaviour is bounded above by the precision with which the behaviour is measured. For most of the twentieth century that measurement was a human observer with a stopwatch and a category scheme, and the resulting data inherited the observer's temporal resolution, attentional limits and expectations.

Manual scoring has three specific defects. It is low-dimensional: a rich, continuous, multi-jointed movement is compressed into a discrete label such as grooming or freezing. It is coarse in time, typically resolving events on the order of hundreds of milliseconds to seconds, while the neural events being related to them occur on the order of milliseconds. And it is theory-laden: the category scheme is chosen before the data are examined, so behaviours that do not fit the scheme are not recorded, and observers who know the experimental condition score ambiguous events in the expected direction.

Markerless pose estimation removes the first two constraints and mitigates the third. A convolutional network is trained on a modest number of human-labelled frames — typically a few hundred — to locate user-defined body parts in video, after which it labels arbitrary quantities of footage at the frame rate of the camera with accuracy comparable to the human labellers. The approach generalises across species and to three-dimensional reconstruction from multiple synchronised cameras.

Categories found rather than imposed

Pose estimation converts video into a numerical time series, and that changes what kind of question can be asked. Rather than counting instances of pre-defined categories, one can embed the postural time series and ask what structure it contains — which recurring movement motifs exist, how long they last, and how they follow one another. Categories recovered this way are answerable to the data, and frequently subdivide or cut across the categories an observer would have imposed. Unsupervised behavioural segmentation methods of this kind require their own citation and are treated in detail in later chapters.

Retrieval practice

1. An experimenter scoring freezing behaviour knows which animals received the drug. Name the bias and give two design changes that remove it.

Show answer
Observer or experimenter-expectancy bias: knowledge of condition systematically shifts how ambiguous events are classified, and the shift is unconscious and not removable by good intentions. Remedies: (i) blind scoring — the scorer receives anonymised, randomly ordered video with no condition information; (ii) automated scoring from pose estimation with the classification criteria fixed in advance, which removes the human from the decision entirely. A useful third measure is to have two independent blinded scorers and report inter-rater reliability.

2. Why does moving from observer-defined categories to data-derived motifs change the kinds of hypothesis that can be tested?

Show answer
Because observer-defined categories can only confirm or fail to confirm structure that was already anticipated — a behaviour without a pre-existing label cannot appear in the data. Data-derived motifs allow discovery of structure nobody posited: a category may split into distinguishable sub-motifs with different neural correlates, or a boundary may fall in an unexpected place. This also permits statements about the sequential organisation of behaviour, since motifs carry timing and transition probabilities that a tally of category counts discards.
08Anatomy

The shape of the human nervous system

By the end of this section you should be able to

  • Divide the nervous system into its central and peripheral components and their subdivisions.
  • Use the standard anatomical planes and directional terms correctly.
  • Explain what an anatomical parcellation is and why several incompatible ones are in use.
  • State why anatomical description must precede functional localisation.

Every functional claim about the human brain is expressed in an anatomical vocabulary, and the vocabulary is a construction rather than a discovery. Its first level — the division into central and peripheral nervous systems, and the subdivision of each — is stable and uncontroversial. Its finer levels are not.

Directional terms

Position is specified relative to the neuraxis, using paired opposites: rostral (toward the nose) and caudal (toward the tail); dorsal (toward the back) and ventral (toward the belly); medial (toward the midline) and lateral (away from it). Sections are taken in three standard planes: sagittal (dividing left from right), coronal (dividing front from back) and axial or horizontal (dividing top from bottom).

One complication is worth fixing early. In animals with a straight neuraxis the directional terms are consistent throughout, but the human neuraxis bends roughly ninety degrees at the midbrain during development. Above the bend, dorsal means toward the top of the head; below it, dorsal means toward the back. This flexure is the reason that dorsal and superior are interchangeable in the forebrain but not in the brainstem or spinal cord.

Parcellation is a decision

Beyond gross anatomy, dividing the cortex into areas requires a criterion, and different criteria yield different maps. Cytoarchitectonic schemes divide by the laminar arrangement of cell bodies; connectivity-based schemes divide by patterns of anatomical or functional coupling; multimodal schemes combine several measures. The resulting parcellations differ in the number of areas and in the placement of borders, and coordinates in one are not straightforwardly translatable into another.

The practical consequence is that a reported location is meaningless without its reference frame: a stereotaxic coordinate requires the template space it was defined in, and an area label requires the atlas it came from. Standard templates and parcellation schemes in common use are discussed with citations in the atlas chapter; for present purposes the point is that anatomical description is an instrument like any other, with a resolution and a set of assumptions.

Retrieval practice

1. A paper reports a result at coordinates (−42, 18, 6) without naming a template space. Why is the location underdetermined?

Show answer
Because a coordinate triple is only meaningful relative to the stereotaxic space it is expressed in, and several are in routine use with different origins and slightly different scaling. The same triple identifies different tissue in different spaces, and the discrepancy is comparable to the size of small cortical areas, so it can move a reported peak across a functional border. Reporting must therefore name the template, and ideally also the parcellation and atlas used to attach an anatomical label.

2. Two parcellations of the same cortex disagree on the number of areas. Is one necessarily wrong?

Show answer
No. They may be dividing by different criteria — cytoarchitecture, connectivity, function, myelin content — which need not produce coincident borders, because a region homogeneous by one measure can be heterogeneous by another. Disagreement is then informative rather than erroneous, indicating that cortical organisation is not fully captured by any single measure. A parcellation is a model of the cortex serving a purpose, and should be evaluated by whether it predicts something independent, not by whether it matches another map.
09Human

Imaging the living brain

By the end of this section you should be able to

  • Trace the development of human brain imaging from radiography to functional MRI.
  • Explain the physical basis of CT, MRI and the BOLD contrast mechanism.
  • Describe the haemodynamic response and its consequences for temporal resolution.
  • State what a functional imaging result does and does not establish.

Every method so far requires access to tissue. Studying the human brain in a living person requires the opposite: measurement from outside the skull, without opening it. The history of that problem is the history of finding physical signals that cross bone.

The first was electrical. Hans Berger's recordings of rhythmic potentials from the human scalp established that the electrical activity of the brain is measurable non-invasively, with a temporal precision — milliseconds — that no subsequent imaging method has matched. Its weakness is inverse: scalp potentials are the summed field of large synchronous populations blurred by the intervening tissue, so the source of a signal is not uniquely recoverable from the recording.

Direct functional mapping was possible only during neurosurgery. Wilder Penfield, stimulating the exposed cortical surface of conscious patients undergoing epilepsy surgery, established the systematic somatotopic organisation of the motor and somatosensory strips — the map later drawn as the homunculus. The method offered direct causal evidence at the cost of being available only in a clinical population with abnormal brains.

Structural imaging arrived through computation rather than through a new signal. X-rays had been used since the 1890s but produce a projection in which overlapping structures are superimposed and soft-tissue contrast is negligible. Godfrey Hounsfield's computerised transverse axial scanning reconstructed a cross-sectional image from many projections taken at different angles, resolving soft tissue for the first time.

Magnetic resonance followed a separate line. Raymond Damadian reported that NMR relaxation times differ between tissues, proposing the difference as a basis for detection; Paul Lauterbur showed that applying magnetic field gradients makes resonance frequency a function of position, so a spatial image can be reconstructed from the signal; and Peter Mansfield developed fast imaging schemes that made acquisition practical. The result is a method with excellent soft-tissue contrast and no ionising radiation.

Functional MRI followed from an observation about the blood rather than about neurons. Seiji Ogawa showed that image contrast depends on blood oxygenation, because deoxyhaemoglobin is paramagnetic and oxyhaemoglobin is not, so local changes in the ratio alter the signal. Since neural activity is followed by a local increase in blood flow that overshoots oxygen consumption, activity produces a measurable signal change. Mapping of human cortical function on this principle followed within two years, first with an injected contrast agent and then without any contrast agent at all.

What the BOLD signal costs

The haemodynamic response is slow. Blood flow changes begin roughly a second after the neural event, peak at about five seconds, and return to baseline over the following ten or more — so the measured signal is the neural time course convolved with a sluggish kernel. Fine temporal structure is irrecoverably smoothed, and events closer together than a few seconds are difficult to separate. The interactive below makes the consequence explicit.

Two further limits deserve emphasis. The BOLD signal is indirect — it measures a vascular consequence of neural activity, not the activity itself, and the coupling between them is neither perfectly linear nor uniform across the brain or across clinical populations. And it is correlational: an activation map shows where the signal covaried with a task, which does not establish that the region performs the associated computation, still less that it is necessary for it. The inferential discipline of Section 6 applies unchanged, with the additional constraint that the causal manipulations available there are largely unavailable in humans.

Retrieval practice

1. EEG resolves milliseconds and fMRI resolves millimetres. Explain the physical reason neither method achieves both.

Show answer
They measure different physical quantities with different intrinsic timescales. EEG measures electrical potential, which propagates essentially instantaneously, so temporal resolution is limited only by sampling — but the potential at the scalp is a summed, volume-conducted field, and recovering its sources is an ill-posed inverse problem, which limits spatial resolution. fMRI measures a vascular quantity localised to within millimetres of the active tissue, giving good spatial resolution — but blood flow responds over seconds, so temporal resolution is limited by physiology, not by the scanner. The limits are properties of the signals, not engineering shortfalls.

2. A region activates during a task. List three distinct explanations besides the region performing the task's computation.

Show answer
(i) It receives a copy of task-related information without acting on it — activity is a consequence of the computation, not its site. (ii) It supports a process correlated with the task but not specific to it, such as arousal, attention, motor preparation or effort, all of which vary with task difficulty. (iii) The signal is non-neural in origin — task-locked changes in respiration, heart rate or head motion produce artefacts that mimic activation. A fourth possibility is that the region is engaged only under the particular comparison used, so the result reflects the baseline condition chosen rather than the task.

    The neuron as a battery

    A nerve cell holds its interior about seventy millivolts below the fluid around it, and spends most of its energy budget maintaining that difference. Signalling consists of briefly, locally, and reversibly throwing it away.

    01Phenomenon

    Two kinds of electrical signal

    By the end of this section you should be able to

    • Distinguish graded potentials from action potentials by amplitude, duration and decay.
    • Explain what "all-or-none" means and what it does not mean.
    • State how signal magnitude is encoded once amplitude is fixed.
    • Identify which signals are suited to local computation and which to long-distance transmission.

    Neurons produce electrical signals of two functionally distinct kinds, and the distinction is not one of degree. Graded potentials — receptor potentials at sensory endings, synaptic potentials at postsynaptic membranes — vary continuously with the strength of whatever produced them, sum with one another, and decay with distance. Action potentials do none of these things.

    A graded potential is an analogue quantity. Double the stimulus and the response roughly doubles; deliver two stimuli close together in time or space and the responses add. This makes graded potentials the natural currency of integration: a neuron summing thousands of synaptic inputs is performing an analogue computation, and the graded nature of those inputs is what makes summation meaningful.

    An action potential is not an analogue quantity. Below a threshold membrane potential, no action potential occurs at all. Above it, an action potential occurs whose amplitude and time course are essentially independent of the stimulus that triggered it. A stimulus twice the threshold does not produce a larger spike. Figure 2.1 lets you locate the transition directly.

    Two corollaries follow, and both matter for everything in later chapters. First, since amplitude carries no information, magnitude must be encoded some other way — principally in firing rate and in the timing of spikes. Second, because the action potential is regenerated at full amplitude at each point along the axon, it does not decay: it is the only one of these signals that can carry information over macroscopic distances. Why that regeneration is necessary is the subject of the next section.

    Retrieval practice

    1. "All-or-none" is often misread as meaning that a neuron either fires or is silent. What does the term actually refer to, and why does the misreading matter?

    Show answer
    It refers to the amplitude of a single action potential being independent of the strength of the stimulus that evoked it, once threshold is crossed — not to the neuron's overall output being binary. The misreading matters because a neuron's output is in fact richly graded: it varies continuously in firing rate, in the temporal pattern of spikes, and in the total number of spikes emitted. Only the size of each individual event is fixed. Treating the cell as a binary device discards precisely the dimensions in which its output actually carries information.

    2. A sensory ending produces a graded receptor potential; the axon leaving it produces action potentials. Why does the nervous system use both rather than one throughout?

    Show answer
    Because the two signal types solve different problems. Graded potentials preserve analogue magnitude and sum linearly, which is what integration of many inputs requires — an all-or-none signal cannot be summed meaningfully. But they attenuate with distance and are useless beyond a millimetre or so. Action potentials sacrifice amplitude information to gain indefinite propagation without loss, which is what transmission requires. The nervous system therefore integrates in the graded domain, converts to the all-or-none domain for transmission at the trigger zone, and converts back at the synapse.
    02Cable

    The problem of distance

    By the end of this section you should be able to

    • Explain why an axon is a poor cable and what physically causes the loss.
    • Define the length constant and state what determines it.
    • Predict how axon diameter and myelination change passive spread.
    • Justify why regeneration, rather than better insulation alone, is required.

    Inject current into an axon at one point and the voltage change spreads away from that point, diminishing as it goes. It diminishes because the axon is a leaky cable: current flowing longitudinally through the axoplasm meets resistance, and at every point along the way some of it escapes across the membrane instead of continuing.

    The formal treatment is cable theory, imported from nineteenth-century telegraphy and applied to nerve by Hodgkin and Rushton, who measured the four electrical constants of a crustacean axon and showed the passive spread of voltage follows the expected exponential form. Steady-state voltage falls off as

    Passive spread
    V(x) = V0 · e−x/λ,    λ = √(rm)√(ri + ro)
    V(x) voltage at distance x from the injection site · λ the length constant, the distance over which the voltage falls to 1/e ≈ 37% of its initial value · rm membrane resistance per unit length · ri, ro axial resistance of the axoplasm and the external medium per unit length

    The structure of that expression is the whole argument. Anything that raises membrane resistance — myelin, principally — lengthens λ. Anything that lowers axial resistance — a wider axon, since resistance falls with cross-sectional area — also lengthens λ. But λ appears in an exponential, and no biologically achievable value of λ makes an exponential stop decaying. For a typical unmyelinated axon λ is on the order of a millimetre. A signal crossing a metre of nerve would be attenuated by a factor of e1000.

    The resolution is not better insulation but active regeneration. If the membrane can be made to generate current in response to depolarisation, rather than merely conducting it away, then a signal arriving attenuated at some point can be restored to full amplitude there and sent on. That is what an action potential is: a booster station, repeated along the axon at intervals shorter than λ. The remainder of this chapter is an account of how the membrane does it.

    Retrieval practice

    1. Two unmyelinated axons differ only in diameter, one being four times wider. How does the length constant differ, and why is the relationship not linear?

    Show answer
    λ scales with the square root of diameter, so a fourfold increase in diameter roughly doubles λ. The reason is that the two resistances in the expression scale differently with radius. Axial resistance ri depends on the cross-sectional area, so it falls as the square of the radius. Membrane resistance per unit length rm depends on circumference, so it falls only linearly with radius. Since λ = √(rm/ri), the ratio grows in proportion to radius and λ grows as its square root. Widening an axon is therefore an expensive way to buy conduction distance — volume rises as the square of radius while λ rises as its square root.

    2. Why can't a neuron simply increase membrane resistance enough to make passive conduction sufficient over a metre?

    Show answer
    Because the decay is exponential, not linear, so improving λ buys only logarithmic gains in usable distance. Carrying a signal one metre with acceptable attenuation would require a λ of the same order — roughly a thousandfold larger than that of a typical unmyelinated axon — which would demand a millionfold increase in the rm/ri ratio, since λ goes as its square root. No membrane approaches that resistance, and even if one did, the associated increase in time constant would make the signal unusably slow. Regeneration escapes the problem entirely by resetting the amplitude repeatedly rather than trying to preserve it.
    03Chemistry

    Gradients, and what they cost

    By the end of this section you should be able to

    • State the direction and approximate magnitude of the principal ionic gradients.
    • Explain the role of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase in maintaining them.
    • Distinguish the gradient as a store of energy from the membrane potential itself.
    • Explain why impermeant intracellular anions matter.

    All electrical signalling in neurons draws on a single stored resource: unequal concentrations of a few small ions across the plasma membrane. The membrane potential is not itself the store. It is what appears when the membrane becomes selectively permeable to an ion that is out of equilibrium.

    The pattern is consistent across species. Potassium is concentrated inside the cell; sodium, chloride and calcium are concentrated outside. Calcium is the most extreme, with a gradient of roughly four orders of magnitude, which is why it is so useful as an intracellular signal. Squid axon values, the ones on which the classical work was done, differ numerically from mammalian values but not in direction.

    Gradients of this size do not arise spontaneously and do not persist without work. The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase hydrolyses ATP to export three sodium ions and import two potassium ions per cycle, against both concentration gradients. This pump accounts for a substantial fraction of the brain's resting energy consumption. The pump is electrogenic — it moves net positive charge outward — but its direct contribution to the resting potential is small; its essential role is to maintain the gradients on which everything else depends.

    The distinction is worth stating carefully because it is a common source of confusion. Blocking the pump does not abolish the membrane potential immediately. The gradients are large, and the number of ions that must move to change the voltage is tiny — as the next section demonstrates quantitatively — so a neuron with its pump inhibited continues to signal for some time, running down the store rather than losing it at once.

    One further asymmetry deserves note. A substantial fraction of the intracellular anionic charge is carried by proteins, phosphates and other organic species that cannot cross the membrane at all. Their impermeance is structural: they balance intracellular cation charge without themselves being able to redistribute, which constrains the equilibrium the mobile ions can reach.

    Retrieval practice

    1. A neuron's Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase is inhibited. Predict the immediate and the eventual consequences for the resting potential, and explain the difference in timescale.

    Show answer
    Immediately, very little happens. The resting potential is set by the existing gradients and the membrane's selective permeability, not by the pump's own current, and the pump's direct electrogenic contribution is only a few millivolts. Over time, however, each action potential and each leak current moves ions down their gradients with nothing restoring them, so [Na⁺] rises inside and [K⁺] falls. As the gradients collapse the equilibrium potentials converge, the resting potential depolarises toward zero, and excitability is lost. The timescale difference exists because the charge needed to set the voltage is minute compared with the total ionic content of the cell — the store is large relative to the rate at which signalling depletes it.

    2. Why is the calcium gradient so much steeper than the others, and what does that buy the cell?

    Show answer
    Intracellular free calcium is held near 10⁻⁴ mM against an external concentration of order 1–10 mM — roughly a ten-thousandfold difference — maintained by pumps, exchangers and intracellular buffering and sequestration. The benefit is signal-to-noise. Because the resting level is so low, opening even a small number of calcium channels produces a very large fractional change in local concentration, which can be read directly by calcium-binding proteins as a signal. An ion held at a high resting concentration could not act as a trigger in this way, because the fractional change on influx would be negligible.
    04Capacitance

    How little charge it takes

    By the end of this section you should be able to

    • Explain why the lipid bilayer behaves as a capacitor.
    • Calculate the charge required to change membrane potential by a given amount.
    • Explain why signalling does not measurably change bulk ion concentrations.
    • Relate membrane capacitance to the speed with which voltage can change.

    A lipid bilayer is about five nanometres of insulator between two conducting salt solutions. That is the definition of a capacitor, and it has a consequence that is easy to state and hard to believe until it is calculated: the number of ions that must cross the membrane to produce an action potential is a vanishing fraction of the ions present.

    Specific membrane capacitance is close to 1 µF/cm² for essentially all biological membranes, a value fixed by the thickness and dielectric constant of the bilayer and used unchanged in the Hodgkin–Huxley model. The charge required to shift the potential across a capacitor is Q = C·ΔV, and dividing by Faraday's constant converts charge to moles of monovalent ion.

    Ions required for a voltage change
    fraction of internal K+ displaced = 2 · Cm · ΔVF · [K+]i · r
    Cm specific membrane capacitance, ≈1 µF/cm² · ΔV the voltage change · F Faraday's constant, 96 485 C/mol · [K⁺]i internal potassium concentration · r axon radius. The factor of two arises because a cylinder's surface-to-volume ratio is 2/r.

    Two conclusions follow. First, an action potential is an electrical event, not a chemical one: bulk concentrations are unchanged by it, which is why a poisoned axon can fire thousands of times before its gradients measurably run down. Second, the relevant charge sits in a thin shell immediately against the membrane. The interior of the cell remains electrically neutral; the potential difference exists across the bilayer, not throughout the cytoplasm.

    Capacitance also sets a speed limit. Changing the voltage requires moving charge, and moving charge takes time, so the membrane responds to a step of current with an exponential rise governed by the time constant τ = rmcm. Large capacitance means slow voltage changes. This is the second reason myelin helps: by placing many bilayers in series it reduces capacitance per unit area as well as raising resistance.

    Retrieval practice

    1. A student argues that repeated firing must quickly deplete intracellular potassium. Using Figure 2.4, explain the error.

    Show answer
    The charge required to swing the membrane potential is set by the membrane's capacitance, which is tiny, whereas the ion reservoir is set by the cell's volume, which is comparatively enormous. For a large axon the fraction of internal potassium displaced per spike is of order one part in ten million, so millions of action potentials would be needed to make an appreciable dent even with no pump running. The error is treating the voltage change as if it required equilibrating the whole cytoplasm, when it only requires charging a thin capacitor. Note the fraction rises as radius falls — in very fine unmyelinated axons the effect is no longer negligible and ionic accumulation becomes physiologically real.

    2. Myelin both raises membrane resistance and lowers membrane capacitance. Explain how each change independently speeds conduction.

    Show answer
    Raising resistance reduces current leaking out across the internodal membrane, so more of the injected current flows longitudinally down the axon and the length constant λ increases — depolarisation reaches the next node with more amplitude. Lowering capacitance reduces the charge needed to change the voltage at each point, so the time constant τ falls and the local voltage changes faster for the same current. Together the signal travels further per unit charge and rises faster on arrival, so successive nodes reach threshold sooner. Neither effect alone would be as effective: high resistance with high capacitance would give a signal that spreads far but rises sluggishly.
    05Nernst

    Equilibrium for one ion

    By the end of this section you should be able to

    • Explain the two opposing forces that define electrochemical equilibrium.
    • Apply the Nernst equation and interpret its terms, including valence and temperature.
    • Calculate equilibrium potentials from concentration data.
    • Explain why the equilibrium potential is independent of permeability.

    Consider a membrane permeable to potassium alone, with potassium concentrated inside. Potassium leaves down its concentration gradient. But potassium carries charge, so each ion that leaves makes the interior slightly more negative, and that negativity pulls potassium back. Equilibrium is reached when the two forces balance exactly — not when the concentrations equalise, which never happens.

    The potential at which this balance occurs is the equilibrium potential for that ion, given by the Nernst relation. Bernstein's membrane hypothesis proposed exactly this arrangement for the resting nerve: a membrane selectively permeable to potassium, with the resting potential accordingly near EK.

    The Nernst equation
    Eion = RTzF ln [ion]out[ion]in
    R gas constant · T absolute temperature · z valence of the ion · F Faraday's constant · concentrations outside and inside. At 20 °C, for a monovalent cation, RT/zF · ln(·) becomes approximately 58 mV · log10([ion]out/[ion]in).

    Three properties of this relation are worth fixing. It is logarithmic: a tenfold change in the ratio shifts the equilibrium potential by about 58 mV at room temperature, so large voltage changes require large concentration changes. It depends on valence, both in magnitude — a divalent ion produces half the potential for the same ratio — and in sign, since for an anion the relation inverts. And it is independent of permeability: the equilibrium potential for an ion is defined by its gradient alone, whether the membrane is highly permeable to it or entirely impermeable.

    That last point is the one students most often lose. Permeability determines how strongly an ion influences the membrane potential; it has no bearing on that ion's own equilibrium potential. A membrane with no sodium permeability still has a sodium equilibrium potential of about +55 mV. It simply has no way of moving toward it.

    Retrieval practice

    1. External potassium is raised from 20 mM to 200 mM while internal potassium stays at 400 mM. By roughly how much does EK change, and in which direction?

    Show answer
    The ratio out:in goes from 20/400 = 0.05 to 200/400 = 0.5 — a tenfold increase. Since a tenfold change in the ratio corresponds to about 58 mV for a monovalent cation at room temperature, EK shifts about 58 mV in the depolarising direction, from roughly −75 mV to roughly −17 mV. This is the basis of the classic experiment demonstrating that the resting potential follows EK: plotting resting potential against log external potassium yields a line of slope ≈58 mV per decade over the range where potassium permeability dominates.

    2. Why does the equilibrium potential not depend on how permeable the membrane is to the ion?

    Show answer
    Because the equilibrium potential is defined as the voltage at which the electrical force on the ion exactly cancels the chemical force from its concentration gradient, and both of those forces are properties of the gradient and the charge, not of the pathway. Permeability affects only the rate at which the system approaches that balance and how strongly this ion competes with others in setting the actual membrane potential. Formally, permeability does not appear anywhere in the Nernst equation — it enters only in the Goldman equation, where several ions with different permeabilities must be weighted against one another.
    06Goldman

    More than one permeant ion

    By the end of this section you should be able to

    • Explain why the Nernst equation alone cannot give the resting potential.
    • Apply the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation and interpret permeability ratios.
    • Explain why chloride and sodium appear inverted in the expression.
    • Predict how a change in relative permeability moves the membrane potential.

    Real membranes are permeable to several ions at once, and no single equilibrium potential can then be satisfied. The membrane settles instead at a compromise: a potential at which the net current summed across all permeant species is zero, though no individual species is at equilibrium and each continues to leak.

    The compromise is weighted by permeability. An ion to which the membrane is highly permeable pulls the membrane potential strongly toward its own equilibrium potential; an ion to which it is nearly impermeable is almost ignored. The quantitative form is the constant-field equation derived by Goldman and applied to nerve in permeability terms by Hodgkin and Katz.

    The Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz voltage equation
    Vm = RTF ln PK[K+]o + PNa[Na+]o + PCl[Cl−]iPK[K+]i + PNa[Na+]i + PCl[Cl−]o
    Px the permeability of the membrane to species x — only the ratios matter, not absolute values. Note that chloride's inside and outside terms are exchanged relative to the cations, because its charge is negative.

    The figure contains the chapter's central insight, and it is worth pausing on. Nothing in the GHK expression changes during an action potential except the permeability ratios. The concentrations are fixed — we established in Section 4 that they barely move. The equilibrium potentials are therefore fixed too. The entire event consists of the membrane changing which ion it listens to.

    Hodgkin and Katz demonstrated this directly by manipulating external sodium and showing that both the action potential's amplitude and its rate of rise depended on it, as they must if the overshoot reflects a transient dominance of sodium permeability. The complementary demonstration came later, when Baker, Hodgkin and Shaw succeeded in extruding the axoplasm and perfusing the axon with artificial solutions, showing that the membrane potential tracks the internal ionic composition as the theory requires.

    Retrieval practice

    1. Why do the chloride terms appear inverted relative to sodium and potassium in the GHK equation?

    Show answer
    Because chloride carries negative charge. For a cation, movement from outside to inside carries positive charge inward and depolarises; for an anion, the same physical movement carries negative charge inward and hyperpolarises. The equation handles this by exchanging the inside and outside terms for anions rather than by carrying a separate sign, which is algebraically equivalent to inverting the concentration ratio as the valence z = −1 would do in the Nernst equation. A useful check: an anion with the same out:in ratio as a cation has an equilibrium potential of the same magnitude and opposite sign.

    2. The GHK equation contains only permeability ratios. What does that imply about a membrane whose permeabilities all double simultaneously?

    Show answer
    The membrane potential is unchanged, because multiplying every P term by the same factor cancels between numerator and denominator. What changes is the membrane's conductance, and therefore its input resistance and time constant — the cell becomes electrically "leakier", responding to a given synaptic current with a smaller voltage change and settling faster. This is the mechanism of shunting inhibition: opening channels can suppress a neuron's responsiveness without shifting its resting potential at all, which is invisible to a measurement of voltage alone.
    07Preparation

    The animal that made it possible

    By the end of this section you should be able to

    • Explain why the squid giant axon was necessary for the classical experiments.
    • Describe what the voltage clamp measures and why it was required.
    • Explain how sodium and potassium currents were separated experimentally.
    • Read a voltage-clamp record and identify each current component.

    The account in the previous section could not have been established without an axon large enough to put an electrode inside lengthwise. J. Z. Young's description of the cephalopod giant fibre supplied it. At up to a millimetre in diameter, the squid giant axon could accept an internal electrode of around 100 µm without micropipettes — a preparation with no equivalent in vertebrates.

    The first direct consequence was the measurement of the resting and action potentials from inside the fibre, published by Hodgkin and Huxley in 1939. The result was immediately awkward for Bernstein's hypothesis. Bernstein had predicted that the action potential represented a transient breakdown of selective permeability, which should drive the membrane potential toward zero. The recorded action potential did not stop at zero. It overshot, reversing the potential to positive values — behaviour requiring not a loss of selectivity but the acquisition of a new selectivity, for an ion with a positive equilibrium potential.

    Working independently, Cole and Curtis showed that membrane conductance rises sharply during the impulse while capacitance stays constant, establishing that the event is a permeability change rather than a structural collapse.

    Why a clamp was needed

    Measuring the permeability changes was harder than detecting them, because the system is circular: permeability depends on voltage, and voltage depends on permeability. Any depolarisation opens channels, which changes the current, which changes the voltage, which changes the permeability again. Nothing can be held still long enough to characterise.

    The voltage clamp breaks the loop. Electronic feedback holds membrane potential at an experimenter-chosen value by injecting whatever current is needed to oppose the membrane's own currents. The injected current is then a direct, sign-inverted read-out of the membrane current at a known, constant voltage. The technique was developed by Cole and Marmond in the late 1940s — a citation deliberately withheld here, as the original method papers could not be resolved to verifiable records.

    The separation of the two components was achieved by exploiting their different dependencies. Replacing external sodium abolishes the early inward current and leaves the late outward one intact, so subtraction yields each in isolation. Their kinetics differ characteristically: the sodium current activates rapidly and then inactivates despite the maintained depolarisation, while the potassium current activates more slowly and is sustained for as long as the step lasts. That sodium conductance depends on voltage in two opposing ways — activation and inactivation — was treated as a separate finding in its own right.

    Retrieval practice

    1. Explain precisely what problem the voltage clamp solves that a current-clamp recording cannot.

    Show answer
    It breaks the positive-feedback loop between voltage and conductance. In current clamp the experimenter sets the current and the membrane's own conductances then determine the voltage, which in turn alters those conductances — so voltage and permeability change together and neither can be measured as a function of the other. Under voltage clamp the voltage is held constant by feedback, so conductance can be studied at a fixed, known driving force, and the injected current directly reports the membrane current. Only then can conductance be extracted as g = I/(V − Eion) and its time course and voltage dependence characterised.

    2. In Figure 2.7, step the command potential above +55 mV. What happens to the early current and why?

    Show answer
    The early current, normally inward, becomes outward. Sodium channels still open — activation depends on depolarisation and is if anything stronger — but current direction depends on the driving force (V − ENa), not on whether channels are open. Above ENa ≈ +55 mV that term changes sign, so open sodium channels now carry sodium out. This is the defining test of ionic identity: the potential at which a current reverses identifies the permeant ion, and it is how the early current was shown to be carried by sodium.
    08Mechanism

    The ionic basis of the action potential

    By the end of this section you should be able to

    • Describe the sequence of conductance changes underlying an action potential.
    • Explain the role of positive feedback in the rising phase and what terminates it.
    • Interpret the gating variables m, h and n and their different time courses.
    • State what the Hodgkin–Huxley model does and does not claim about mechanism.

    The final paper of the 1952 series assembled the measured conductances into a set of coupled differential equations and integrated them numerically — by hand-cranked mechanical calculator — to reconstruct an action potential that had never been fitted to. The reconstruction matched the recorded waveform in amplitude, time course, threshold, refractory behaviour and conduction velocity. It remains one of the most successful quantitative models in biology.

    The Hodgkin–Huxley membrane equation
    Cm dVdt = I − ḡNam3h(V − ENa) − ḡKn4(V − EK) − ḡL(V − EL)
    m sodium activation, fast · h sodium inactivation, slower, and falling with depolarisation · n potassium activation, slow. Each obeys dx/dt = αx(V)(1−x) − βx(V)x. Squid parameters: Cm 1 µF/cm²; ḡNa 120, ḡK 36, ḡL 0.3 mS/cm²; at 6.3 °C, with all rates scaled by φ = 3(T−6.3)/10.

    The sequence, in words

    Depolarisation increases m, opening sodium channels. Sodium entry depolarises further, which increases m further: this is regenerative positive feedback, and it is what makes the upstroke both explosive and all-or-none. Threshold is simply the potential at which inward sodium current first exceeds outward potassium and leak current, so that the feedback becomes self-sustaining.

    Two processes terminate the upstroke, and both are slower than activation by design. Sodium inactivation — the fall of h — closes sodium channels despite the maintained depolarisation. Simultaneously the slower rise of n opens potassium channels, and potassium efflux repolarises the membrane. Because n remains elevated briefly after the potential has returned to rest, the membrane transiently overshoots in the hyperpolarising direction: the afterhyperpolarisation.

    It matters what the model does not claim. Hodgkin and Huxley were explicit that their equations were an empirical description of the measured currents, not a demonstration of molecular mechanism; the exponents 3 and 4 were chosen because they fitted the kinetics, not because the existence of independent gating particles had been shown. The molecular structures that justify them retrospectively were unknown for decades. The model's authority rests on its predictive success, and the 1963 Nobel Prize recognised the ionic-mechanism work as such.

    Retrieval practice

    1. Both sodium inactivation (h falling) and potassium activation (n rising) oppose the upstroke. If a drug abolished inactivation but left potassium conductance intact, what would the action potential look like?

    Show answer
    It would still repolarise, because the delayed potassium conductance alone can eventually outweigh the sodium current, but the spike would be markedly prolonged — sodium channels would remain open throughout the depolarisation, so repolarisation would only proceed once n had risen far enough to dominate, producing a broad plateau-like waveform. Recovery would also be impaired: since inactivation normally imposes the absolute refractory period, its removal would leave the cell able to re-excite much sooner, and repetitive or unstable firing would be likely. This is essentially the phenotype of toxins and mutations that impair sodium-channel inactivation.

    2. Explain why the action potential is all-or-none in terms of the m-dependent feedback loop, referring to threshold.

    Show answer
    Sodium activation is regenerative: depolarisation increases m, which increases sodium influx, which further depolarises. Whether that loop runs away depends on whether inward sodium current exceeds the outward potassium and leak currents opposing it. Below threshold it does not, the outward currents dominate, and the membrane relaxes back — the response is graded and proportional to the stimulus. Above threshold the loop becomes self-sustaining and proceeds to completion regardless of what triggered it, so the amplitude is set by ENa and the model's own kinetics rather than by the stimulus. Threshold is thus not a property of any channel but the balance point of two opposing currents.
    09Propagation

    Form, refractoriness and speed

    By the end of this section you should be able to

    • Name the phases of the action potential using standard nomenclature.
    • Distinguish absolute from relative refractory periods and state their mechanisms.
    • Explain how refractoriness enforces unidirectional propagation and limits firing rate.
    • Relate conduction velocity to axon diameter and myelination.

    The waveform has a standard vocabulary. The rising phase or upstroke carries the potential from threshold to its peak; the portion above 0 mV is the overshoot, the feature that falsified Bernstein's prediction. The falling phase returns the potential toward rest, and the afterhyperpolarisation or undershoot carries it transiently below rest before recovery.

    Following a spike the membrane is temporarily less excitable, in two distinguishable stages. During the absolute refractory period no stimulus of any size can evoke a second action potential, because sodium channels are inactivated — h is near zero — and inactivation is removed only by repolarisation and time. During the relative refractory period a second spike is possible but requires a larger stimulus, because h has recovered only partially while n remains elevated, so more inward current is needed to overcome the residual potassium conductance.

    Refractoriness is not merely a limitation. It performs two functions. It enforces unidirectional propagation: the membrane behind an advancing action potential is refractory, so the impulse cannot reverse and travels only forward. And it sets a ceiling on firing rate, since the absolute refractory period defines a minimum interspike interval and therefore a maximum frequency at which the cell can encode.

    Speed

    Conduction velocity depends on how quickly local circuit current can bring the next stretch of membrane to threshold, so it improves with anything that raises λ or lowers τ. In unmyelinated axons this means diameter, with velocity increasing approximately as the square root of diameter — a poor return, since volume rises as its square.

    Myelination changes the mechanism rather than the parameters. Wrapping the axon in many layers of membrane raises resistance and lowers capacitance along the internode, while voltage-gated channels are concentrated at the nodes of Ranvier. Excitation therefore jumps from node to node — saltatory conduction, demonstrated by Huxley and Stämpfli in single myelinated fibres. For myelinated fibres velocity becomes approximately proportional to diameter rather than to its square root, and Rushton showed how fibre size governs the relationship.

    The comparison sets the squid's solution in context. A 0.5 mm unmyelinated axon conducts at roughly 25 m/s. A myelinated vertebrate fibre achieves the same velocity at a diameter some fiftyfold smaller, and the fastest reach far higher. Nothing about the squid's arrangement is optimal; it is the outcome available to an animal whose lineage lacks myelin, and it happened to be exactly the preparation nerve physiology required.

    Retrieval practice

    1. Explain, in terms of the gating variables, why the absolute refractory period cannot be overcome by a larger stimulus while the relative refractory period can.

    Show answer
    During the absolute refractory period sodium inactivation h is near zero, so almost no sodium channels are available to open regardless of how strongly m is driven — the conductance term ḡNam³h is close to zero because of the h factor, and a bigger stimulus cannot recruit channels that are inactivated. Recovery of h requires repolarisation and time, not current. During the relative refractory period h has partially recovered, so some sodium conductance is available; the obstacle is now the residual elevated n, which opposes depolarisation with outward potassium current. That is a quantitative obstacle rather than an absolute one, so a larger inward current can overcome it and reach threshold.

    2. A myelinated axon is demyelinated by disease while its nodal channels remain intact. Predict the effect on conduction and explain the mechanism.

    Show answer
    Conduction slows markedly and may fail altogether. Losing myelin lowers internodal membrane resistance and raises capacitance, so current leaks out across the internode instead of flowing to the next node, and more charge is needed to change the local voltage. The depolarisation arriving at the next node is therefore both smaller and slower to develop, and if it no longer reaches threshold, conduction blocks. The bare internodal membrane also lacks the density of voltage-gated sodium channels needed to regenerate the impulse continuously, since those were concentrated at nodes — so the axon can neither conduct saltatorily nor fall back on continuous conduction.

      01Quantal

      The signal arrives in packets

      By the end of this section you should be able to

      • Describe miniature end-plate potentials and explain their significance.
      • State the quantal hypothesis and the evidence that established it.
      • Apply Poisson statistics to estimate quantal content from failures.
      • Explain why lowering external calcium was the decisive experimental manoeuvre.

      By the mid-1930s it was established that transmission at the neuromuscular junction is chemical: stimulating the motor nerve releases acetylcholine at the endings, and the released transmitter accounts for the muscle's response. What remained entirely open was the form in which transmitter left the terminal — as a continuously graded secretion, or in some quantised unit.

      The answer arrived, as such answers often do, from an artefact nobody was looking for. Recording intracellularly from the frog end-plate with the nerve at rest, Fatt and Katz found small depolarisations occurring spontaneously and at random, roughly one per second, each about half a millivolt. They were confined to the end-plate region, they were abolished by curare and potentiated by prostigmine — the pharmacology of acetylcholine exactly — and their amplitudes formed a smooth bell-shaped distribution with a coefficient of variation near thirty per cent. The terminal, doing nothing, was leaking transmitter in discrete parcels.

      The inference was immediate and audacious. If the resting terminal releases single packets, perhaps the evoked response is nothing but many of those same packets released at once. Testing that required making the evoked response small enough to count, which del Castillo and Katz achieved by lowering external calcium and raising magnesium until the end-plate potential collapsed from its normal seventy or eighty millivolts to a few millivolts or nothing at all.

      Under those conditions the evoked response behaves in a way no continuous-secretion model can produce. Individual trials fail entirely, with some frequency. When they succeed, the amplitudes cluster at integer multiples of the spontaneous miniature amplitude. And the whole distribution — the failures, the peak positions, the relative peak heights — is predicted by a single parameter, the mean number of packets released per trial.

      Quantal statistics in the low-calcium regime
      P(k quanta) = mk e−mk!,    so   m = ln NtotalNfailures
      m mean quantal content, the average number of packets released per stimulus · k the number released on a given trial. When m is small the release is Poisson, and the proportion of complete failures alone is enough to estimate m — a prediction that can be checked against the amplitude histogram computed independently.

      That last identity is what made the argument compelling rather than merely suggestive. The failure rate and the mean amplitude are separate measurements, and Poisson statistics forces them into agreement. The same treatment was extended to mammalian muscle shortly afterwards, where the multi-peaked histograms are particularly clean. Run Figure 5.1 with m below about one and the failure bar dominates; raise it toward five and the peaks merge into a smooth distribution as the discreteness is buried by summation.

      At normal external calcium, quantal content at the neuromuscular junction is high — of order a hundred or more — and release is better described by binomial statistics with a finite number of release sites, each with some probability of releasing. The Poisson description is the limiting case, valid when the probability per site is low. The historical importance of the low-calcium condition is exactly that it moves the synapse into the regime where the underlying discreteness becomes visible.

      Retrieval practice

      1. In 200 trials at low external calcium, 74 produce no response at all. Estimate the mean quantal content, and state what independent measurement you would use to check it.

      Show answer
      Using m = ln(Ntotal/Nfailures) = ln(200/74) = ln(2.70) ≈ 1.0, so about one quantum per stimulus on average. The independent check is the amplitude method: measure the mean amplitude of all evoked responses including failures, measure the mean amplitude of spontaneous miniature potentials, and take the ratio. If the Poisson description holds, the two estimates should agree. Their agreement is the substance of the quantal argument, because the failure count and the amplitude ratio are logically independent measurements that a continuous-secretion model gives no reason to reconcile.

      2. Why does lowering external calcium reveal quantal structure that is invisible at normal calcium?

      Show answer
      Because discreteness is only observable when the number of units per trial is small. At normal calcium each stimulus releases of order a hundred quanta or more, and by the central limit theorem the sum of that many jittered units is a smooth, near-Gaussian distribution with no resolvable steps — the granularity is buried. Lowering calcium reduces release probability so that trials contain zero, one, two or three quanta, and at those counts the individual steps and the outright failures are directly resolvable. The underlying mechanism is unchanged; only the sampling regime differs.
      02Comparison

      Two ways to cross a gap

      By the end of this section you should be able to

      • Contrast electrical and chemical synapses in structure, speed and function.
      • Explain what the synaptic delay reveals about the underlying mechanism.
      • Describe rectification at an electrical synapse.
      • State what chemical transmission buys at the cost of speed.

      Chemical transmission is not the only option, and for a period it was not the favoured one. The dispute — caricatured as soup versus sparks — was settled not by choosing a winner but by finding that both mechanisms exist, in different places, doing different jobs.

      Furshpan and Potter, recording on both sides of the crayfish giant motor synapse, found current passing directly from pre- to postsynaptic cell with no measurable delay, and passing preferentially in one direction. The junction behaved as a rectifier: depolarising current crossed readily in the orthodromic direction and poorly in the reverse. This was an electrical synapse, and its structural basis is the gap junction, a paracrystalline array of channels linking the cytoplasms of the two cells directly.

      Electrical synapses are not a curiosity confined to invertebrates. They are widespread in the mammalian brain, particularly among inhibitory interneurons, where connexin-36-based coupling supports synchronisation of firing across populations.

      The delay is the diagnostic feature and the reason it matters is worth stating precisely. Electrical continuity implies no delay: current spreads as fast as it can flow. A delay of several tenths of a millisecond therefore excludes direct electrical continuity, and it is not accounted for by diffusion across the cleft, which is far too rapid at that distance. Katz and Miledi showed that the delay is dominated by the time required for calcium channels to open and for calcium to act on the release machinery.

      What does chemical transmission buy in exchange for that delay? Three things an electrical synapse cannot provide. Amplification: a small presynaptic terminal can trigger a large postsynaptic response, because the energy comes from the postsynaptic cell's own gradients rather than being passed through. Sign inversion: a chemical synapse can be inhibitory, whereas current passing directly between coupled cells cannot change sign. And modulability: a chemical synapse has many independently regulable steps — synthesis, packaging, release probability, receptor density, reuptake — which is what makes it a substrate for plasticity.

      Retrieval practice

      1. A recording shows a postsynaptic response beginning 0.1 ms after the presynaptic spike. Is this synapse electrical or chemical, and what single further test would confirm it?

      Show answer
      Almost certainly electrical: chemical transmission requires calcium channels to open and the release machinery to act, which costs several tenths of a millisecond at minimum, so 0.1 ms is too fast. The decisive confirmatory test is removal of extracellular calcium, or blockade of presynaptic calcium channels. Chemical transmission is abolished; electrical coupling persists, because it requires only open gap-junction channels and an electrical driving force. A useful secondary test is injecting subthreshold current presynaptically — an electrical synapse transmits graded subthreshold signals, a chemical synapse generally does not.

      2. Electrical synapses are faster and structurally simpler. Why has chemical transmission become the dominant mode in the vertebrate brain?

      Show answer
      Because speed is rarely the binding constraint, and the properties chemical synapses add are exactly those computation requires. Electrical coupling passes an attenuated copy of the presynaptic signal, so it cannot amplify, cannot invert sign, and offers little scope for independent regulation — coupled cells tend simply to synchronise. Chemical synapses amplify, so a small terminal can drive a large cell; they can be excitatory or inhibitory, which is a precondition for any circuit that computes rather than merely spreads activity; and their many separable stages give numerous sites at which strength can be modified, which is the physical basis of learning.
      03Trigger

      Calcium is the trigger

      By the end of this section you should be able to

      • State the relationship between external calcium and transmitter release.
      • Interpret the cooperativity exponent and explain what it implies mechanistically.
      • Explain why calcium entry, not depolarisation itself, is the proximate trigger.
      • Describe the spatial and temporal scale over which calcium acts.

      Depolarisation of the terminal is necessary for release but is not the trigger. The trigger is calcium, and the evidence is that a terminal depolarised in the absence of external calcium releases nothing, while calcium admitted at the right moment produces release with a latency of well under a millisecond.

      The dependence on calcium concentration is steep and it is steep in an informative way. Dodge and Rahamimoff varied external calcium at the neuromuscular junction and plotted quantal content against it on logarithmic axes, obtaining a straight line of slope close to four — a mean of 3.78 with a standard deviation of 0.2 across twenty-eight experiments.

      Cooperative dependence on calcium
      quantal content  m  ∝  [Ca2+]on,    n ≈ 3.78 ± 0.2
      A power-law exponent near four implies that release requires the near-simultaneous binding of about four calcium ions to the sensor. The consequence is extreme steepness: a doubling of calcium entry produces roughly a fourteenfold increase in release, so small changes in calcium influx translate into large changes in synaptic strength.

      Cooperativity of about four is a strong constraint on any proposed mechanism. It says the calcium sensor is not a simple one-to-one switch but a molecule binding several ions, with release occurring only when the site is substantially occupied. Section 6 identifies the molecule that satisfies this description.

      The steepness also explains the spatial arrangement of the terminal. Because release depends on roughly the fourth power of local concentration, and because cytoplasmic calcium is heavily buffered, only vesicles very close to an open calcium channel experience a concentration high enough to trigger fusion. Release is therefore driven by transient local calcium microdomains near channel mouths rather than by the bulk terminal concentration, which is why the coupling distance between channel and vesicle is itself a determinant of release probability.

      Retrieval practice

      1. External calcium is reduced to half its normal value. Predict the effect on quantal content, and explain why the answer is not "halved".

      Show answer
      Release falls by a factor of about 23.78 ≈ 14, so quantal content drops to roughly 7% of control — not to 50%. The relationship is a power law with exponent near four, not a proportionality, because release requires the cooperative binding of several calcium ions and the probability of achieving that scales as the concentration raised to the number of sites. This is exactly the nonlinearity that makes the low-calcium condition such a practical experimental tool: a modest reduction in calcium moves the synapse from hundreds of quanta per trial into the countable regime of Figure 5.1.

      2. Why does fourth-power cooperativity imply that the distance between a calcium channel and a docked vesicle matters so much?

      Show answer
      Because calcium concentration falls steeply with distance from an open channel — cytoplasmic buffers and diffusion confine the high-concentration domain to tens of nanometres — and release depends on roughly the fourth power of that concentration. A vesicle twice as far from the channel therefore experiences a much lower concentration and a disproportionately, roughly sixteen-fold-plus, lower release probability. Coupling distance becomes a design variable: synapses that need high release probability place channels tightly against docked vesicles, while loose coupling yields low-probability, more modulable release.
      04Structure

      One quantum, one vesicle

      By the end of this section you should be able to

      • State the vesicle hypothesis and the morphological evidence for it.
      • Describe the quick-freeze experiment that linked exocytosis to quanta.
      • Give the dimensions and molecular composition of a synaptic vesicle.
      • Explain what "one quantum, one vesicle" does and does not assert.

      The quantal hypothesis of Section 1 is a statistical claim; it says nothing about what a quantum physically is. Electron microscopy supplied the candidate almost simultaneously, when small membrane-bounded vesicles were found clustered at presynaptic terminals in the mid-1950s. The original electron-microscopic identification of synaptic vesicles appears in work published as a meeting abstract and in early ultrastructural papers whose bibliographic records could not be verified for this edition, so they are described here without a citation rather than with a guessed one.

      Correlating vesicles with quanta required catching fusion in the act. Heuser and colleagues stimulated the frog neuromuscular junction and froze it within milliseconds, then used freeze-fracture to visualise the presynaptic membrane. Vesicle openings appeared alongside the active zone, peaked five to six milliseconds after the stimulus, and — decisively — the number of openings counted scaled with the number of quanta discharged electrophysiologically in the same preparation. A quantum is the contents of one vesicle.

      The composition is remarkable for how crowded it is. A vesicle roughly forty nanometres across carries, on this analysis, about seventy copies of synaptobrevin, thirty-two of synaptophysin, fifteen of synaptotagmin 1, ten of Rab3A, eight synapsins and around a dozen neurotransmitter transporters. Its transmitter cargo at the neuromuscular junction is of the order of five to ten thousand acetylcholine molecules. This is not a passive container; it is a densely instrumented machine whose surface is largely occupied by the apparatus of its own trafficking.

      One caution about the phrase "one quantum, one vesicle". It asserts that the unit of release corresponds to the contents of a single vesicle. It does not assert that every vesicle contains identical cargo — the thirty per cent coefficient of variation in miniature amplitude shows they do not — nor that the postsynaptic response to a quantum is fixed, since that depends equally on receptor number and state. Quantal size is a property of the synapse as a whole, presynaptic and postsynaptic together.

      Retrieval practice

      1. A manipulation halves the mean miniature amplitude. Name two mechanistically distinct explanations and an experiment that distinguishes them.

      Show answer
      Presynaptic: each vesicle is loaded with less transmitter — for example through impaired vesicular transporter function or reduced cytoplasmic transmitter — so the same receptor population sees a smaller pulse. Postsynaptic: fewer or less responsive receptors, so the same quantum of transmitter produces a smaller current. They are distinguished by testing the postsynaptic cell's response to transmitter applied directly, bypassing release entirely — by exogenous agonist application or fast uncaging. If the response to applied transmitter is unchanged, the change is presynaptic; if it is halved, it is postsynaptic. Changes in the coefficient of variation and in quantal content are informative secondary measures.

      2. Why was the quick-freeze experiment more decisive than simply observing vesicles in resting terminals?

      Show answer
      Because the mere presence of vesicles at terminals is correlative — it establishes that vesicles exist near release sites, not that their fusion constitutes the quantum. The quick-freeze experiment introduced the missing element: temporal resolution sufficient to catch fusion events, in a preparation where the number of quanta released could be counted electrically at the same time. Finding that the number of exocytotic profiles scaled with the number of quanta converted an anatomical association into a quantitative identity between one fusion event and one quantum.
      05Traffic

      The vesicle cycle

      By the end of this section you should be able to

      • Describe the stages of the synaptic vesicle cycle in order.
      • Explain the evidence that vesicle membrane is recycled locally.
      • Distinguish the readily releasable pool from the reserve pool.
      • Explain why local recycling is a physical necessity for a terminal.

      A terminal firing at ten hertz and releasing a hundred quanta per impulse consumes a thousand vesicles a second. It holds nowhere near enough to sustain that, and it is often a metre or more from the cell body, far beyond the reach of any synthetic supply line operating on the relevant timescale. Vesicles must therefore be rebuilt where they are used.

      Heuser and Reese demonstrated this by stimulating the frog neuromuscular junction in the presence of an extracellular tracer and following where the tracer went. It first appeared in cisternae and coated vesicles, and later in synaptic vesicles themselves — membrane that had been added to the surface during exocytosis was being retrieved and reassembled into new vesicles locally.

      Vesicles within a terminal are not interchangeable. A small readily releasable pool is docked at the active zone and primed for immediate fusion; a larger reserve pool is tethered further back, mobilised only during sustained activity. The distinction has direct physiological consequences: depletion of the readily releasable pool underlies short-term synaptic depression during high-frequency firing, and the rate at which it is refilled sets how quickly a synapse recovers.

      Retrieval is fast but not instantaneous, and it is the step that limits sustained transmission. Its speed is why terminals can fire continuously for long periods without exhausting their membrane, and its finite rate is why they eventually depress.

      Retrieval practice

      1. A synapse depresses strongly during a 20 Hz train but recovers fully within seconds. Explain both observations in terms of vesicle pools.

      Show answer
      Depression reflects depletion of the readily releasable pool: at 20 Hz, vesicles are consumed faster than docking and priming can replace them, so the number available to fuse — and therefore quantal content — falls with successive stimuli. Recovery over seconds reflects refilling of that pool, by mobilisation of reserve-pool vesicles and by endocytosis and reuse of retrieved membrane, both of which proceed on a timescale of hundreds of milliseconds to seconds. The two timescales are separate parameters: the depression rate is set by release probability and pool size, the recovery rate by the refilling machinery.

      2. Why must vesicle membrane be retrieved locally rather than returned to the cell body?

      Show answer
      Because of the mismatch between distance and rate. Axonal transport moves material at a few hundred millimetres per day at best, so a round trip from a distal terminal takes days, while a terminal consumes vesicles thousands of times per second. There is also a membrane-area problem: exocytosis continuously adds vesicle membrane to the terminal surface, and without local retrieval the terminal would swell and the active zone's organisation would be destroyed within seconds. Local recycling solves both — it conserves the scarce components and holds surface area constant.
      06Molecular

      The fusion machine

      By the end of this section you should be able to

      • Name the core SNARE proteins and describe the complex they form.
      • Explain how SNARE assembly supplies the energy for membrane fusion.
      • Identify the calcium sensor and the evidence for that identification.
      • Relate the molecular model back to the cooperativity measured in Section 3.

      Two lipid bilayers do not fuse spontaneously. Bringing them close enough requires displacing ordered water and overcoming electrostatic repulsion, an energetic barrier that must be paid for. The machine that pays it is a set of proteins identified through the biochemistry of intracellular membrane traffic rather than through neurophysiology.

      Söllner and colleagues, purifying the proteins that bind the fusion factor NSF and its adaptor, recovered a small set of membrane proteins — syntaxin and SNAP-25 on the target membrane, synaptobrevin on the vesicle — and proposed that the pairing of vesicle and target SNAREs supplies the specificity of membrane targeting. Reconstitution into artificial liposomes later showed that these proteins alone are sufficient to drive fusion.

      The structure explains the mechanism. The SNARE complex is a parallel four-helix bundle: one helix from synaptobrevin, one from syntaxin, and two from SNAP-25, wound together along their lengths. Because synaptobrevin is anchored in the vesicle and the others in the plasma membrane, zippering the bundle from its far end toward the membranes pulls the two bilayers together. Assembly is thermodynamically favourable, and the energy released is what forces the membranes into contact.

      Zippering alone would fuse membranes as soon as the proteins met, which is not what synapses do — they hold vesicles primed and release them within a fraction of a millisecond of a calcium signal. The missing element is a calcium sensor that clamps the reaction and then releases the clamp.

      Synaptotagmin is that sensor, and the case for it was assembled in three steps. It was first characterised as a vesicle protein with domains homologous to the regulatory region of protein kinase C, capable of binding phospholipid; it was then shown to bind calcium and proposed explicitly as the sensor on the vesicle surface; and finally genetic deletion in mice established it as the major calcium sensor for fast release at a central synapse. Its calcium-binding C2 domains were solved structurally in the same period.

      This closes the loop with Section 3. A sensor with multiple calcium-binding sites, all of which must be substantially occupied before the clamp releases, is exactly what a power-law dependence with an exponent near four demands. The physiology predicted the molecule's binding stoichiometry roughly three decades before the molecule was found. The identification of the general vesicle-trafficking machinery was recognised by the 2013 Nobel Prize.

      Retrieval practice

      1. Clostridial toxins cleave SNARE proteins. Predict the effect on spontaneous miniature events and on evoked release, and explain any difference.

      Show answer
      Both are suppressed, because both depend on SNARE-mediated fusion — this is what identifies the SNAREs as core fusion machinery rather than merely regulatory. Evoked release is typically abolished more completely and at lower toxin exposure, because it demands rapid, synchronous fusion of primed vesicles, which requires substantially intact SNARE complexes. A residual trickle of spontaneous events can persist when a small fraction of complexes remains functional, since spontaneous fusion has no timing requirement. The dissociation is quantitative rather than mechanistic.

      2. Explain how a physiological measurement made in 1967 constrained the properties of a protein not identified until the 1990s.

      Show answer
      The fourth-power dependence of release on calcium is a statement about binding stoichiometry, independent of molecular identity. It says the trigger requires roughly four calcium ions bound near-simultaneously, so whatever the sensor turned out to be, it had to possess multiple cooperative calcium-binding sites and had to be positioned where it experiences the transient high concentration near a channel. Synaptotagmin, with its tandem calcium-binding C2 domains anchored in the vesicle membrane, satisfies both constraints. This is a clean illustration of how a quantitative physiological law can specify molecular properties in advance of the molecule.
      07Postsynaptic

      Reading the transmitter

      By the end of this section you should be able to

      • Distinguish ionotropic from metabotropic receptors by mechanism and timescale.
      • Define the reversal potential and explain how it identifies the permeant ions.
      • Explain why the end-plate current reverses near zero millivolts.
      • Read a synaptic current–voltage relation.

      Transmitter in the cleft is a chemical signal; the postsynaptic cell's currency is electrical. The conversion is performed by receptors, and they come in two architectures with very different consequences for speed.

      Ionotropic receptors are themselves ion channels: transmitter binding opens a pore directly, so the response begins within a fraction of a millisecond and lasts milliseconds. Metabotropic receptors are not channels; binding activates a G protein, which acts on effectors and second messengers, which in turn modulate channels elsewhere in the membrane. The response is slower by orders of magnitude — hundreds of milliseconds to minutes — but it is amplified and can alter the cell's state broadly rather than opening a single conductance. A modern review of receptor classes and their signalling is needed here.

      For an ionotropic receptor the essential measurement is the reversal potential: the membrane potential at which the synaptic current changes direction. It identifies the permeant ions, because a channel permeable to a single species reverses at that species' equilibrium potential, and a channel permeable to several reverses at a weighted intermediate value.

      Takeuchi and Takeuchi made this measurement at the end-plate and found that the acetylcholine-activated current reverses near zero millivolts — far from the equilibrium potential of either sodium or potassium alone. The channel is permeable to both, so it drives the membrane toward a value between ENa and EK. The same logic applies to the glutamatergic channels that carry most fast excitation in the central nervous system.

      The reversal potential is the single most useful number to know about a synaptic conductance, because together with the membrane potential it determines both the size and the sign of the response. That is the subject of the next section.

      Retrieval practice

      1. A synaptic current reverses at −70 mV. Which ion most likely carries it, and what would you measure to confirm?

      Show answer
      Chloride, since −70 mV is close to ECl in a typical neuron; the likely receptors are GABA-A or glycine. Potassium is the alternative to exclude, since EK is also negative, though usually nearer −90 mV. The confirmatory test is to change the ion's equilibrium potential and see whether the reversal potential follows: alter intracellular chloride, for instance by changing the pipette solution, and the reversal potential should shift by the amount the Nernst equation predicts. A current carried by potassium would be unaffected by that manipulation but would shift with external potassium.

      2. Why does a channel permeable to both sodium and potassium reverse near 0 mV rather than at ENa or EK?

      Show answer
      Because the reversal potential of a multi-ion channel is the potential at which the net current through it is zero — the point where inward sodium flux and outward potassium flux exactly cancel. With comparable permeability to both, that balance point lies between ENa (≈ +60 mV) and EK (≈ −90 mV), and lands near 0 mV. Neither ion is at its own equilibrium there: both continue to flow, in opposite directions and in equal measure. This is the same weighted-compromise logic as the Goldman equation, applied to a single channel type rather than to the whole membrane.
      08Sign

      Excitation, inhibition and shunting

      By the end of this section you should be able to

      • Define excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic potentials in terms of threshold.
      • Calculate driving force and predict how it changes with membrane potential.
      • Explain shunting inhibition and why it can be invisible in a voltage recording.
      • Explain why a synapse's sign is not a fixed property of its transmitter.

      Whether a synapse excites or inhibits is not determined by its transmitter, nor by the direction it moves the membrane potential in isolation. It is determined by the relationship between its reversal potential and the threshold for firing.

      Synaptic current
      Isyn = gsyn · (Vm − Erev)
      gsyn the conductance opened by the transmitter · (Vm − Erev) the driving force. Transmitter binding controls only the conductance. The size and sign of the resulting current are set by how far the membrane sits from the reversal potential.

      An excitatory postsynaptic potential moves the membrane toward threshold; the mixed-cation channels of Section 7, reversing near 0 mV, do this from any physiological resting potential. An inhibitory postsynaptic potential moves it away, or holds it where it is. Coombs, Eccles and Fatt showed that the inhibitory potential in spinal motoneurones is produced by a selective increase in chloride conductance and reverses accordingly.

      The figure makes the counter-intuitive case concrete. When the resting potential sits below ECl, opening chloride channels depolarises the cell — yet the synapse remains inhibitory, because it drives the membrane toward ECl and ECl is below threshold. A depolarising inhibitory synapse is not a contradiction. Inhibition means clamping the membrane below threshold, not moving it downward.

      The limiting case is shunting inhibition, where ECl equals the resting potential. The driving force is zero, so no current flows and no voltage deflection appears. The synapse is nonetheless powerfully inhibitory, because the added conductance lowers the input resistance: any concurrent excitatory current now produces a smaller voltage change, since ΔV = I·R and R has fallen. This is inhibition that is invisible to a voltage recording at rest and only reveals itself when the cell is challenged — a direct application of the point in Chapter 2 that the Goldman equation depends on permeability ratios, so scaling all conductances changes responsiveness without changing potential.

      The practical consequence is that a synapse's sign is contingent. It depends on the chloride gradient, which is set by transporters whose expression changes during development and can change in disease. GABAergic transmission is depolarising in immature neurons and becomes hyperpolarising as chloride transporter expression shifts — the same transmitter, the same receptor, opposite effect.

      Retrieval practice

      1. A GABAergic synapse produces no measurable voltage change at rest. Your colleague concludes it is non-functional. Give a better explanation and a test.

      Show answer
      ECl probably equals the resting potential, so the driving force is zero and no net current flows even though the conductance opens normally — classic shunting inhibition, and a potent one. The test is to measure input resistance, or the size of a concurrent excitatory response, during the GABAergic input: both should fall markedly if the conductance is real. Equivalently, hold the cell away from ECl with a voltage or current clamp and the synaptic current will appear, with its direction depending on which side of ECl you hold.

      2. Explain how a depolarising synaptic response can be inhibitory.

      Show answer
      Because inhibition is defined relative to threshold, not relative to the resting potential. If ECl lies above the resting potential but below threshold, opening chloride channels depolarises the cell toward ECl — yet it simultaneously clamps the membrane at that value, so any excitatory input now has to work against a conductance pulling toward a subthreshold potential. The added conductance also shunts. The net effect is to make firing less likely despite the depolarisation, which is why immature GABAergic synapses can be depolarising and still restrain activity.
      09Integration

      Summation and the decision to fire

      By the end of this section you should be able to

      • Distinguish temporal from spatial summation and state what governs each.
      • Explain the role of the membrane time constant in integration.
      • Explain why the trigger zone is the site of the decision to fire.
      • Evaluate the evidence for astrocytic participation at the synapse.

      A single excitatory synapse in the central nervous system produces a postsynaptic potential of a fraction of a millivolt — perhaps a hundredth of what is needed to reach threshold. No individual input decides anything. Firing is a collective outcome, and the operation that produces it is summation.

      The two modes are not equivalent, and Figure 5.9 makes the asymmetry plain. A train arriving at a single synapse converges on a ceiling: each new potential adds to a residue that is itself decaying, so the sum forms a geometric series with a finite limit, and at realistic amplitudes that limit falls well short of threshold. No amount of firing at one synapse will discharge the cell. Spatial convergence has no such ceiling — simultaneous inputs add without decay between them — which is why threshold is reached by recruiting many synapses rather than by driving one harder.

      Temporal summation combines inputs arriving in succession at the same synapse. It works because a synaptic potential decays with the membrane time constant τ = RmCm; if a second input arrives before the first has decayed, the two add. The time constant therefore sets the width of the window over which a neuron integrates. Spatial summation combines inputs arriving simultaneously at different synapses, and is governed by how well each spreads passively to the trigger zone — the cable problem of Chapter 2, with the length constant λ setting the reach. Typical values of the membrane time constant and length constant in central neurons require citation to a cable-theory reference.

      The decision is made at the trigger zone, the region of the axon initial segment where voltage-gated sodium channel density is highest and threshold is consequently lowest. Everything upstream is analogue and graded; everything downstream is the all-or-none signal of Chapter 2. The neuron's computation is the conversion between them, and its parameters — where synapses sit, how far they are from the trigger zone, what the time constant is, how inhibition is distributed — are what a circuit designer has to work with.

      A third element?

      The account above involves two cells. The tripartite synapse hypothesis proposes a third: astrocytes, whose processes envelop many synapses, sense transmitter, respond with internal calcium signals, and release gliotransmitters that feed back on synaptic function.

      This should be presented as genuinely contested rather than settled, and it is worth being explicit about why. Astrocytic calcium signalling and gliotransmission are well documented in culture and in slice preparations. What remains disputed is whether astrocytes release transmitter to modulate synapses under physiological conditions in the intact brain: results from mice lacking the principal astrocytic calcium-release pathway have been largely negative, concerns have been raised that findings in cultured cells may not generalise, and some calcium-imaging observations are attributable to methodological artefact. The concept has been influential and the anatomy is not in doubt; the physiological significance of gliotransmission in vivo is an open question, and a textbook that reports it as established is misrepresenting the state of the field.

      Retrieval practice

      1. A neuromodulator halves the membrane time constant. Predict the effect on temporal and on spatial summation, and explain the difference.

      Show answer
      Temporal summation is impaired: each synaptic potential decays twice as fast, so the window during which a second input can add to the first is halved, and a train that previously reached threshold may no longer do so. Spatial summation is much less affected, since simultaneous inputs add regardless of decay rate — though a shorter time constant usually accompanies a lower input resistance, which reduces the amplitude of every response. Functionally, the cell becomes a coincidence detector rather than an integrator: it now responds to inputs arriving together and ignores those merely arriving in sequence.

      2. Two excitatory synapses produce equal responses when recorded at the soma, but one is on a distal dendrite and one on the soma. What must differ, and why?

      Show answer
      The distal synapse must generate a larger local response, because passive spread attenuates it on the way to the soma — its signal decays over the dendrite according to the length constant, so equal amplitudes at the soma imply unequal amplitudes at source. The difference could arise from a larger conductance, more receptors, greater release probability, or from dendritic voltage-gated channels amplifying the local response en route. This is the phenomenon of synaptic scaling by location, and it is why measuring synaptic strength at the soma alone can be misleading about what is happening at the synapse.
