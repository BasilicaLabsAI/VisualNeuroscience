# Precentral Gyrus — Functions, Receptors and Evidence

Oct 17, 2026 · @Fadil Karim

## At a glance

The precentral gyrus is the brain's main motor strip. Its rear part, primary motor cortex (M1), sends the commands for voluntary movement of the opposite side of the body; its front part, lateral premotor cortex, chooses and prepares them. It also steers the eyes, drives the muscles of speech, voice, swallowing and breathing, helps lay down new motor skills, and responds when people imagine, watch or hear actions. A network described in 2023, the somato-cognitive action network (SCAN), threads through it; its discoverers propose that it links movement to arousal and body physiology. Chemically it is one of the most receptor-sparse parts of the cortex, apart from a high density of α1 adrenoceptors, which the original receptor study tied to premotor cortex ([open it in the Region Atlas](https://visualneuroscience.ai/regions.html#sel=P%3APrecentral)).

## Anatomy and boundaries

The AAL Precentral region is the strip of cortex between the central sulcus behind and the precentral sulcus in front, one region per hemisphere (Precentral\_L, Precentral\_R). AAL drew its regions by hand on the MNI single-subject brain, using the main sulci as landmarks ([Tzourio-Mazoyer et al., 2002](https://doi.org/10.1006/nimg.2001.0978)). The strip holds two architectonic territories: primary motor cortex (Brodmann area 4) at the back and lateral premotor cortex (area 6) at the front ([Visual Neuroscience, BA4](https://visualneuroscience.ai/regions.html#sel=B:4); [BA6](https://visualneuroscience.ai/regions.html#sel=B:6)).

- **Primary motor cortex (BA4, M1).** Human area 4 divides into an anterior area 4a and a posterior area 4p on cytoarchitecture and transmitter-receptor binding, and each holds its own finger representation. Area 4p was more active during roughness discrimination than during self-generated movement ([Geyer et al., 1996](https://doi.org/10.1038/382805a0)).
- **"Old" and "new" M1.** In macaques, the caudal part of M1 contains cortico-motoneuronal cells that synapse directly onto spinal motoneurons; the rostral part has none. The caudal "new M1" is present only in some higher primates and humans ([Rathelot & Strick, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0808362106)).
- **Premotor cortex (BA6).** The front of the gyrus carries the lateral part of area 6. The medial part, the supplementary motor area, is a separate AAL region ([Tzourio-Mazoyer et al., 2002](https://doi.org/10.1006/nimg.2001.0978); [Visual Neuroscience, BA6](https://visualneuroscience.ai/regions.html#sel=B:6)).
- **Hand knob.** The hand area sits on a knob shaped like an omega or epsilon in axial slices and a hook in sagittal ones. It is a reliable MRI landmark for finding the gyrus, even in pathology ([Yousry et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/120.1.141)).
- **Betz cells.** These giant pyramidal neurons sit in layer Vb and make up about one-tenth of its pyramidal cells. They are largest medially and cluster about two-thirds of the way out from the midline ([Rivara et al., 2003](https://doi.org/10.1002/ar.a.10015)).
- **Layer 4.** Brodmann called area 4 "agranular", but a thin layer IV has since been found in adult rhesus monkeys ([García-Cabezas & Barbas, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/ejn.12585)), and neurons with layer-4-like circuitry in mouse motor cortex ([Yamawaki et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.05422); commentary in [Barbas & García-Cabezas, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tins.2015.03.005)).
- **Variability.** Area 4 borders vary between people and between hemispheres. Area 4 itself is symmetric, but the precentral part of the pyramidal tract is larger on the left ([Rademacher et al., 2001](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/124.11.2232)).
- **What the AAL strip leaves out.** The leg and foot representation continues over the top of the hemisphere onto the medial wall ([Penfield & Boldrey, 1937](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/60.4.389)), which AAL assigns to its separate Paracentral Lobule region ([Tzourio-Mazoyer et al., 2002](https://doi.org/10.1006/nimg.2001.0978)).

## Movement execution

The precentral gyrus is the cortex's main launch point for voluntary movement of the opposite side of the body, but it shares the job with premotor areas, and much of its command passes through the spinal cord's own circuits.

- **Shared corticospinal output.** In macaques, fibres to the cervical spinal cord come from M1 and from six premotor areas, whose arm representations together hold as many corticospinal neurons as M1's arm area, or more. Premotor areas make up over 60% of the frontal cortex that projects to the spinal cord, which undercuts the idea of M1 as the "final common pathway" ([Dum & Strick, 1991](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.11-03-00667.1991)).
- **M1's share of the tract.** A recent review puts primary motor cortex at around 30% of corticospinal fibres ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/cne.25567)).
- **Betz cells are a small minority.** The giant Betz cells supply only about 2–3% of corticospinal axons in primates, based on classic fibre counts ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/cne.25567)).
- **Direct lines to motoneurons.** Cortico-motoneuronal connections run in parallel with indirect routes through spinal interneurons. Their influence is significant and may serve special functions, including adaptive use of the hands ([Lemon, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.neuro.31.060407.125547)).
- **Bypassing the spinal cord.** Because cortico-motoneuronal cells sit in the caudal "new M1", that region can bypass spinal integrative circuits and sculpt novel patterns of muscle activity for skilled movement ([Rathelot & Strick, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0808362106)).
- **Where dexterity came from.** Strick and colleagues argue that primate dexterity grew partly through changes in M1's organisation and partly through extra output pathways from other cortical areas, especially the medial motor areas ([Strick et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-neuro-070918-050216)).
- **Head and face.** Corticobulbar fibres from the motor cortex reach the pons and lower brainstem ([Kuypers, 1958](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/81.3.364)).

## Body map

The gyrus maps the opposite side of the body, from the foot near the top of the hemisphere to the tongue near the Sylvian fissure. That map is real but rough: representations overlap, follow how the body is used, and are interrupted by a separate whole-body action network.

- **Discovery.** Electrical stimulation of the dog's frontal cortex moved the limbs on the opposite side ([Fritsch & Hitzig, 1870; English translation 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.yebeh.2009.03.001)). Stimulation mapping in conscious patients then laid out the human sequence ([Penfield & Boldrey, 1937](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/60.4.389)).
- **The textbook order.** Body parts run from the tongue ventrally to the foot dorsally ([Meier et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.90531.2008)).
- **Overlap within a limb.** Each finger, the wrist, elbow and shoulder are represented across much of the arm area, and these representations overlap extensively. Only gradual somatotopic gradients remain ([Schieber, 2001](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.2001.86.5.2125)).
- **Breaks in the order.** High-resolution fMRI found the arm emphasised in two zones, one above and one below a finger-dominated zone ([Meier et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.90531.2008)).
- **Shaped by use.** The similarity between finger activity patterns is stable across people. It is better explained by how hands move in everyday life than by correlated muscle activity ([Ejaz et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.4038)).
- **A map of actions.** Half-second stimulation trains in monkey precentral cortex evoke coordinated multi-joint postures, such as the hand forming a grip and moving to an opening mouth. The sites form a map of hand positions around the body that spans M1 and premotor cortex ([Graziano et al., 2002](<https://doi.org/10.1016/s0896-6273(02)00698-0>)).
- **The whole body in the hand knob.** Multi-unit recordings from the hand knob of people with tetraplegia showed strong tuning to face, head, arm and leg movements. Analogous movements of different limbs, such as a hand grasp and a toe curl, shared part of their neural code ([Willett et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2020.02.043)).
- **The somato-cognitive action network (SCAN).** Precision fMRI shows the homunculus is interrupted by inter-effector regions that alternate with the foot, hand and mouth areas. They have thinner cortex, connect strongly with each other and with the cingulo-opercular network, and co-activate during action planning and axial movements such as of the abdomen or eyebrows ([Gordon et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-05964-2)). The authors call this an integrate–isolate layout: effector areas isolate fine control, while SCAN integrates goals, physiology and whole-body movement (same source).
- **Further support for SCAN.** Within M1, the network has three nodes. Microdissection of 16 hemispheres found three small gyral bridges across the central sulcus (plis de passage) with extra white matter at the node locations, and cortical stimulation during surgery found inter-effector responses there ([Skandalakis et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awaf022)). TMS-EEG in 16 healthy adults found a response specific to the inter-effector site between the hand and leg hotspots at about 42 ms ([Mancuso et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2026.122261)).

## How it codes movement

Precentral neurons carry the ingredients of a movement — force, direction, joint and muscle patterns — but an influential view holds that the population behaves less like a list of parameters than like a dynamical system that prepares a movement and then lets it unfold (see Open questions). Most of this evidence comes from recordings in monkeys.

- **Force.** Pyramidal tract neuron firing tracks the force exerted during voluntary movement ([Evarts, 1968](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.1968.31.1.14)).
- **Direction.** About three-quarters of task-related arm cells (241 of 323) fired in an orderly way with movement direction, mostly as a cosine of the angle from a preferred direction ([Georgopoulos et al., 1982](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.02-11-01527.1982)). Summing each cell's preferred direction, weighted by its activity, gives a "population vector" that points where the arm moves ([Georgopoulos et al., 1986](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.3749885)).
- **Muscles and movements.** In a wrist task that pulled the two apart, 28 of 88 M1 neurons behaved like muscles and 44 of 88 coded the direction of movement in space ([Kakei et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.285.5436.2136)).
- **Preparation.** Between an instruction and the movement it called for, 61% of precentral pyramidal tract neurons changed activity according to the coming push or pull ([Tanji & Evarts, 1976](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.1976.39.5.1062)). Preparatory activity does not leak into the muscles because it cancels out in the population's readout, an "output-null" pattern ([Kaufman et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.3643)).
- **Population dynamics.** During reaching, motor cortex activity shows a brief, strong oscillatory component whose amplitude and phase are set by the preparatory state ([Churchland et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature11129)). This supports a dynamical-systems view, in which the current population state shapes the next ([Shenoy et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-neuro-062111-150509)).
- **Beta rhythm.** Motor cortex produces 20–30 Hz bursts that are coherent with hand-muscle activity, mainly while a grip is held steady ([Baker et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7793.1997.225bo.x)). Beta activity is proposed to signal keeping the current motor state ([Engel & Fries, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.conb.2010.02.015)), and these rhythms desynchronise around movement ([Pfurtscheller & Lopes da Silva, 1999](<https://doi.org/10.1016/s1388-2457(99)00141-8>)).

## Planning and selection

The front half of the gyrus, lateral premotor cortex, turns cues and goals into a chosen action — though preparation is not confined there, because M1 neurons carry preparatory activity too (see How it codes movement).

- **A distinct premotor field.** Monkey premotor neurons relate to voluntary movement as M1's do, but more of them respond to the visuospatial cue, and evoking movement there needs stronger stimulation ([Weinrich & Wise, 1982](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.02-09-01329.1982)).
- **Cue-to-action rules.** After premotor removal, monkeys could not relearn a task in which a visual cue told them which movement to make ([Halsband & Passingham, 1985](<https://doi.org/10.1016/0166-4328(85)90035-x>)).
- **Choosing between options.** In monkeys facing two possible reach targets, 43% of directionally tuned dorsal premotor cells fired for whichever target lay near their preferred direction, so the population signalled both options at once. Once a cue picked one, its signal grew, the other was suppressed, and the activity predicted the choice, including errors ([Cisek & Kalaska, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2005.01.027)).
- **Holding a plan without moving.** In monkeys, dorsal premotor cortex uses the same output-null trick as M1, preparing a movement without triggering it ([Kaufman et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.3643)).
- **Dorsal versus ventral.** Dorsal and ventral premotor areas sit in different cortical networks and handle different aspects of planning and decision-making ([Hoshi & Tanji, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.conb.2007.02.003)).
- **Where they are in humans.** A meta-analysis of 126 imaging papers mapped M1, dorsal and ventral premotor cortex, SMA, pre-SMA and S1 into the Human Motor Area Template used for region-of-interest analysis ([Mayka et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2006.02.004)).

## Eye movements and attention

The human frontal eye field (FEF), a key cortical hub for voluntary gaze ([Vernet et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnint.2014.00066)), sits at the front edge of this gyrus, near where the precentral sulcus meets the superior frontal sulcus ([Paus, 1996](<https://doi.org/10.1016/0028-3932(95)00134-4>)).

- **Location.** Blood-flow studies put the FEF near the precentral sulcus and/or deep in the caudal superior frontal sulcus, rather than in Brodmann area 8 as long assumed. It is consistent front-to-back (Y −6 to 1 mm) and top-to-bottom (Z 44 to 51 mm) but varies side-to-side ([Paus, 1996](<https://doi.org/10.1016/0028-3932(95)00134-4>)). Because that sulcus is an AAL boundary, the FEF can fall in the Precentral region or in its frontal neighbours.
- **Not one eye field.** Where the human FEF appears depends on the method, stimulation or imaging. The evidence suggests at least four frontal eye fields — frontal, supplementary, cingulate and premotor — though a human cingulate eye field has not yet been located ([Amiez & Petrides, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pneurobio.2009.07.010)).
- **What it controls.** The FEF contributes to fixation, saccades, smooth pursuit and vergence, as well as to attentional orienting, visual awareness and perception ([Vernet et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnint.2014.00066)).
- **Attention.** The FEF is part of the dorsal frontoparietal network for goal-directed, top-down selection of stimuli and responses ([Corbetta & Shulman, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn755)).
- **Attention without moving the eyes.** In monkeys, FEF stimulation too weak to trigger a saccade enhanced visual responses in V4 neurons whose receptive fields matched the stimulated site ([Moore & Armstrong, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature01341)).

## Speech, voice, swallowing and breathing

The lower half of the gyrus runs the muscles of speech, and its signals are now clear enough to drive speech neuroprostheses for people who can no longer talk.

- **Articulators.** Lips, jaw, tongue and larynx are mapped somatotopically on the ventral precentral and postcentral gyri. Their activity forms a population code organised by phonetic features, switching between consonants and vowels within tens of milliseconds ([Bouchard et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature11911)).
- **A larynx area.** Vocalising and wordless glottal stops activate the same larynx-specific motor area, proposed as the main vocal centre of human motor cortex ([Brown et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhm131)). In humans this area lies in area 4 with direct links to laryngeal motoneurons, whereas the monkey's sits in premotor area 6 and projects only indirectly ([Simonyan, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.conb.2014.05.006)).
- **Pitch and melody.** Populations in the dorsal laryngeal motor cortex of both hemispheres encode vocal pitch, for emphasis in speech and for singing. Stimulating there evokes laryngeal movement and involuntary vocalisation ([Dichter et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2018.05.016)).
- **The middle precentral gyrus.** This zone, between the hand and mouth areas, supports laryngeal control, auditory processing, reading and writing. Injury to it can cause pure apraxia of speech, and it may handle syllable-level sequencing, a role usually credited to Broca's area ([Silva et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1614-22.2022)).
- **Apraxia of speech.** In 136 people with acute left middle cerebral artery stroke, lesions linked to apraxia of speech centred on the left precentral gyrus ([Itabashi et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1161/STROKEAHA.115.010402)).
- **Speech neuroprostheses.** In a participant with ALS, arrays in ventral premotor area 6v decoded attempted speech at 62 words per minute, with a 9.1% word error rate on 50 words and 23.8% on 125,000 words. Arrays in area 44, part of Broca's area, carried little usable information ([Willett et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06377-x)).
- **Day-one accuracy.** Four arrays in the left ventral precentral gyrus reached 99.6% accuracy on a 50-word vocabulary after 30 minutes of calibration. Accuracy on prompted sentences held at about 97.5%, at about 32 words per minute, over 8.4 months, and he used it in self-paced conversation for more than 248 hours ([Card et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2314132)).
- **A synthetic voice.** In a man with ALS, 256 electrodes in the ventral precentral gyrus drove instant voice synthesis, letting the user change intonation and sing short melodies in real time ([Wairagkar et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09127-3)). Attempted speaking modes and loudness, from miming and whispering to loud speech, can also be decoded there ([Srinivasan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-71284-4)).
- **Swallowing.** TMS mapping in 20 people placed oral, pharyngeal and oesophageal muscles on motor and premotor cortex of both hemispheres, asymmetrically and regardless of handedness. In two stroke patients, dysphagia appeared to go with a smaller pharyngeal map in the undamaged hemisphere, which grew as swallowing recovered ([Hamdy et al., 1996](https://doi.org/10.1038/nm1196-1217)).
- **Breathing.** The diaphragm has its own motor cortical representation. TMS with the coil about 3 cm from the midline on the scalp evoked mainly opposite-side diaphragm responses ([Maskill et al., 1991](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.1991.sp018825)).

## Motor learning and plasticity

The precentral gyrus is not a fixed keyboard: practice reshapes its maps within minutes, it helps consolidate new skills, and it reorganises after injury.

- **Fast and slow learning.** Within the first session of practising a finger sequence, the patch of M1 it activated first shrank, then grew. After four weeks of daily practice the trained sequence activated more M1 than an untrained one, and the change lasted months ([Karni et al., 1995](https://doi.org/10.1038/377155a0)).
- **Minutes of practice.** After 15–30 minutes of practising thumb movements in a new direction, TMS-evoked thumb movements pointed toward the practised direction for several minutes ([Classen et al., 1998](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.1998.79.2.1117)).
- **Early consolidation.** Low-frequency rTMS of M1, but not of other areas, disrupted retention of a newly gained finger skill. Basal motor behaviour and later learning were unaffected ([Muellbacher et al., 2002](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature712)).
- **Synaptic strengthening.** In rats, skill training pushed M1's horizontal synapses toward the top of their modification range, which reduced further LTP and enhanced LTD ([Rioult-Pedotti et al., 2000](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.290.5491.533)). These horizontal connections are proposed as the substrate for map reorganisation ([Sanes & Donoghue, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.neuro.23.1.393)).
- **Musicians.** Keyboard players have a more symmetric precentral hand area than non-musicians, and its size correlates with how early training began ([Amunts et al., 1997](<https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1097-0193(1997)5:3%3C206::AID-HBM5%3E3.0.CO;2-7>)).
- **Dopamine.** In rats, dopamine fibres from the ventral tegmental area to M1 are needed to learn a reaching skill but not to perform one already learned ([Hosp et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.5411-10.2011)). A single 100 mg dose of levodopa sped up formation of a motor memory in young adults and restored it in older ones ([Flöel et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.20536)).
- **Noradrenaline and acetylcholine.** A single dose of the noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor reboxetine improved motor skill acquisition and corticomotor excitability ([Plewnia et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1212/01.wnl.0000128041.92710.17)). In rats, removing basal forebrain cholinergic input abolished M1 map plasticity and impaired skill learning ([Conner et al., 2003](<https://doi.org/10.1016/s0896-6273(03)00288-5>)).
- **Plasticity on demand.** Pairing median-nerve stimulation with TMS induces an LTP-like rise in excitability ([Stefan et al., 2000](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/123.3.572)), blocked by the NMDA-receptor antagonist dextromethorphan ([Stefan et al., 2002](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2002.023317)). Theta-burst stimulation changes M1 physiology strongly and for a long time after only 20–190 seconds ([Huang et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2004.12.033)).
- **Boosting learning.** Anodal tDCS over M1 during five days of training increased skill gains, entirely through between-day consolidation, and the advantage was still present at three months ([Reis et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0805413106)).
- **After injury.** In squirrel monkeys with small infarcts, rehabilitative training prevented further loss of the hand representation and sometimes expanded it, alongside recovery of skilled hand use ([Nudo et al., 1996](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.272.5269.1791)).

## Beyond movement

The gyrus also activates when people imagine, watch, hear, read about or silently rehearse actions, and part of it may link movement to the body's arousal systems. How much of this is necessary for thought, rather than a by-product, is still argued over (see Open questions).

- **Simulated action.** Intending, imagining and observing actions activate the motor system below the threshold for movement, which Jeannerod proposed as a simulation mechanism ([Jeannerod, 2001](https://doi.org/10.1006/nimg.2001.0832)). Across 75 imaging papers, imagery reliably recruited a fronto-parietal network, but primary motor cortex itself was not consistently active ([Hétu et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2013.03.017)).
- **Watching actions.** Watching someone grasp objects enlarged TMS-evoked hand-muscle responses in the same pattern as performing the grasp ([Fadiga et al., 1995](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.1995.73.6.2608)). Watching mouth, hand and foot actions activated premotor cortex somatotopically ([Buccino et al., 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11168545/)), part of the proposed mirror-neuron system ([Rizzolatti & Craighero, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.neuro.27.070203.144230)).
- **Hearing speech.** Listening to syllables activated a superior part of ventral premotor cortex that overlaps a speech-production area on the border of areas 4a and 6 ([Wilson et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1263)). Heard lip and tongue sounds activated the matching lip and tongue zones of the gyrus ([Pulvermüller et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0509989103)), and TMS over those zones selectively changed discrimination of lip versus tongue sounds ([D'Ausilio et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2009.01.017)).
- **Reading action words.** Reading "lick", "pick" or "kick" activated motor-strip zones next to or overlapping those for moving the tongue, fingers or feet ([Hauk et al., 2004](<https://doi.org/10.1016/s0896-6273(03)00838-9>)).
- **Inner speech.** In four participants with arrays along the precentral gyrus, inner speech was represented, most strongly in ventral area 6v and area 55b. In three of them, imagined sentences were decoded in real time, with word error rates of 14–33% on a 50-word vocabulary. A neural "motor-intent" signal separates it from attempted speech, and the authors tested ways to stop a device decoding private inner speech ([Kunz et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2025.06.015)).
- **Rhythm.** Listening to musical rhythms recruited mid-premotor cortex, the SMA and cerebellum even when people did not expect to tap along. Dorsal premotor cortex responded when actions had to be chosen by higher-order rules of timing; ventral premotor cortex only when listening was coupled to action ([Chen et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhn042)).
- **Sense of limb movement.** Tendon vibration that creates an illusion of the arm extending, with no muscle activity, activated area 4a, area 4p and dorsal premotor cortex. The authors concluded that motor areas help convey the felt movement ([Naito et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.19-14-06134.1999)).
- **Cognitive tasks.** A meta-analysis of imaging experiments that reported M1 activation in six kinds of cognitive task, with button presses excluded, found each engaged a different part of M1; left area 4a was shared by four of the six ([Tomasino & Gremese, 2016](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2016.00298)).
- **Mind–body links.** In primates, all frontal motor areas and parts of somatosensory cortex have multisynaptic access to the adrenal medulla, the gland that releases adrenaline ([Dum et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1605044113)). In human resting-state fMRI, SCAN's inter-effector nodes are strongly functionally connected with the cingulo-opercular network, which handles action, physiological control, arousal, errors and pain ([Gordon et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-05964-2)).

## Receptor density profile

Compared with most of the cortex, the precentral gyrus is receptor-sparse, with one striking exception: α1 adrenoceptors, where only the supplementary motor area is denser. The figures come from the Visual Neuroscience receptor layer, which keeps two open datasets apart ([receptor charts](https://visualneuroscience.ai/regions.html#receptors)).

- **Post-mortem autoradiography:** absolute densities for 15 receptors, measured in tissue sections from three brains, cortex only ([Zilles & Palomero-Gallagher, 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnana.2017.00078)).
- **In vivo PET:** 19 receptors and transporters compiled from published tracer studies ([Hansen et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-022-01186-3)). Each tracer has its own binding measure, so a PET value is compared across regions, not against other receptors. The chart below shows 18 of them: the layer lists no precentral value for the nineteenth, 5-HT4.

&#91;embedded content: Visual Neuroscience receptor layer · ex vivo autoradiography (Zilles & Palomero-Gallagher 2017), three post-mortem brains · 15 receptors\]

Across the cortical regions in the autoradiography data, the precentral gyrus ranks second for α1 adrenoceptors, behind only the supplementary motor area (1,880 fmol/mg protein) ([Visual Neuroscience](https://visualneuroscience.ai/regions.html#receptors)). The original study placed the α1 maximum in premotor area 6, where high α1 helps separate premotor from primary motor cortex, so the precentral value may owe much to the gyrus's premotor strip ([Zilles & Palomero-Gallagher, 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnana.2017.00078)). Receptor binding also separates the two subdivisions of area 4, 4a and 4p ([Geyer et al., 1996](https://doi.org/10.1038/382805a0)).

&#91;embedded content: Visual Neuroscience receptor layer · in vivo PET (Hansen et al. 2022 compilation) · 18 targets, share of each target's densest of 45 regions\]

In vivo, the precentral gyrus sits outside the 20 densest of 45 regions for 16 of these 18 targets. The exceptions are the noradrenaline transporter (8th) and α4β2 nicotinic receptors (14th) ([Visual Neuroscience](https://visualneuroscience.ai/regions.html#receptors)). Read with the α1 result, this may point to a noradrenergic emphasis in motor cortex — a pattern in the data, not an established finding.

The precentral and postcentral gyri come out within 5% of each other for 8 of the 18 PET targets, yet differ clearly in autoradiography. For telling motor from sensory cortex apart, the tissue measurements are the sharper guide.

This fits the original autoradiography study, which found the smallest summed receptor fingerprint of its 44 cortical areas in area 4, with motor areas 4 and 6 contrasting with every sensory area ([Zilles & Palomero-Gallagher, 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnana.2017.00078)).

**Why the chemistry matters here.** A TMS pulse over this gyrus produces a measurable muscle response, so drug effects on its receptors can be tested in living people ([Ziemann et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinph.2014.08.028)).

- **GABA.** Drugs that boost GABA, such as vigabatrin and baclofen, reduce intracortical excitability on paired-pulse TMS without changing motor threshold ([Ziemann et al., 1996](https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.410400306)).
- **NMDA.** The LTP-like rise in excitability after paired associative stimulation is blocked by the NMDA-receptor antagonist dextromethorphan ([Stefan et al., 2002](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2002.023317)).
- **Noradrenaline.** The noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor reboxetine increases motor-cortex excitability and intracortical facilitation, leaving motor threshold and intracortical inhibition unchanged ([Plewnia et al., 2002](<https://doi.org/10.1016/s0304-3940(02)00803-0>)).
- **Dopamine and acetylcholine.** D1 and D2 receptors are scarce here in the PET data above, yet dopamine and acetylcholine inputs both matter for skill learning (see Motor learning and plasticity).

## Connections

The precentral gyrus gathers plans, body signals and loop feedback, and sends commands to the spinal cord, brainstem, basal ganglia, cerebellum and even the adrenal gland.

&#91;embedded content: Main connections of the precentral gyrus · mostly monkey tracing studies plus human TMS; sources below\]

- **Premotor input.** In monkeys, the premotor areas that send corticospinal fibres also project directly to the M1 arm area ([Dum & Strick, 1991](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.11-03-00667.1991)).
- **Somatosensory input.** In monkeys, area 2 and parts of area 5 connect reciprocally with area 4, and area 3a's connections suggest it is best treated as part of area 4; area 3b has no direct link with it ([Jones et al., 1978](https://doi.org/10.1002/cne.901810206)).
- **Thalamic relays.** In monkeys, dentate (cerebellar) and internal pallidal output reaches M1 somatotopically, but from only about 30% of the dentate and under 15% of the internal globus pallidus ([Hoover & Strick, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.19-04-01446.1999)).
- **Across the midline.** In humans, a conditioning pulse over one motor cortex inhibits the other at intervals of 5–6 ms or longer, most strongly in the hand area ([Ferbert et al., 1992](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.1992.sp019243)).
- **Neuromodulators.** In rats, dopamine from the ventral tegmental area is needed to learn a reaching skill ([Hosp et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.5411-10.2011)), and acetylcholine from the basal forebrain is needed for both skill learning and M1 map plasticity ([Conner et al., 2003](<https://doi.org/10.1016/s0896-6273(03)00288-5>)).
- **Spinal cord and brainstem.** Corticospinal and corticobulbar output is covered under Movement execution ([Dum & Strick, 1991](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.11-03-00667.1991); [Lemon, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.neuro.31.060407.125547); [Kuypers, 1958](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/81.3.364)).
- **Putamen.** In monkeys, area 4 projects to the putamen on both sides, with very few fibres to the caudate ([Künzle, 1975](<https://doi.org/10.1016/0006-8993(75)90384-4>)).
- **Cerebellar loop.** In monkeys, the M1 arm area receives cerebellar input mainly from lobules IV–VI and projects back to the same lobules through the pons ([Kelly & Strick, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.23-23-08432.2003)).
- **Adrenal medulla.** In primates, all frontal motor areas, plus parts of somatosensory cortex, have multisynaptic access to the adrenal medulla; the SMA and cingulate motor areas make up a major part of that network ([Dum et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1605044113)).
- **SCAN's functional connectivity.** In resting-state fMRI, inter-effector nodes are functionally connected with the SMA, dorsal anterior cingulate, dorsolateral putamen and centromedian thalamus, whereas effector zones connect mainly to the opposite M1 and adjacent S1 ([Gordon et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-05964-2)).

## Clinical significance

Damage to the precentral gyrus or its fibres causes some of neurology's most common deficits, and stimulating or recording from it underpins some of its newest treatments.

- **Stroke.** As people with hemiparesis recovered after stroke, hand-grip activation fell in primary motor, premotor and many other regions ([Ward et al., 2003](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awg245)). Strength at 72 hours, TMS of the descending motor pathway and diffusion MRI together predicted 12-week arm recovery, with 88% positive and 83% negative predictive power ([Stinear et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/aws146)). Low-frequency rTMS of the undamaged hemisphere's M1 has Level A (definite) evidence for hand recovery after the acute phase ([Lefaucheur et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinph.2019.11.002)).
- **ALS.** ALS destroys upper and lower motor neurons ([Brown & Al-Chalabi, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMra1603471)). One hypothesis holds that it begins in the cortico-motoneuron, the cell behind the primate cortex's direct links to spinal motoneurons ([Eisen et al., 1992](https://doi.org/10.1002/mus.880150215)). In SOD-1 gene carriers, short-interval intracortical inhibition vanished or fell before symptoms in three people who went on to develop ALS ([Vucic et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awn071)).
- **Seizures.** Hughlings Jackson inferred that the cortex maps the body by watching focal seizures march across it ([York & Steinberg, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awr219)). In 328 people with focal epilepsy, 13.4% had temporary paralysis after a seizure, always on the side opposite the focus, lasting a median of 173.5 seconds ([Gallmetzer et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1212/wnl.62.12.2160)).
- **Dystonia.** Focal hand dystonia involves lost inhibition at cortical, brainstem and spinal levels, which appears to let movement overflow into unwanted spasms ([Hallett, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nbd.2010.08.025)). Writer's cramp shows exaggerated associative plasticity in motor cortex ([Quartarone et al., 2003](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awg273)), and laryngeal and focal hand dystonia both show altered SCAN connectivity ([Wang et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/mds.70021)).
- **Parkinson's disease.** Resting tremor is thought to be triggered by the basal ganglia and produced by the cerebello-thalamo-cortical circuit ([Helmich et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/aws023)). In an imaging dataset of 863 people, Parkinson's showed excess connectivity between SCAN and subcortex; effective treatments reduced it, and aiming TMS at SCAN rather than effector zones doubled its efficacy ([Ren et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-10059-1)). High-frequency rTMS over both M1s has Level B (probable) evidence for motor symptoms ([Lefaucheur et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinph.2019.11.002)).
- **Tourette syndrome.** Deep brain stimulation sites that reduced tics most were more strongly connected with the cingulo-opercular network and SCAN ([Baldermann et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-55242-6)).
- **Pain.** Chronic motor cortex stimulation still helped 8 of 12 people with central pain at one year ([Tsubokawa et al., 1991](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-7091-9160-6_37)). High-frequency rTMS of M1 opposite the painful side has Level A evidence for neuropathic pain ([Lefaucheur et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinph.2019.11.002)).
- **Brain–computer interfaces.** A 96-electrode array in M1 let a man with tetraplegia move a cursor, open e-mail and work a prosthetic hand ([Hochberg et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature04970)). Later systems gave robotic reach and grasp, with one participant drinking coffee from a bottle ([Hochberg et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature11076)), and seven-dimensional prosthetic arm control at 91.6% success ([Collinger et al., 2013](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(12)61816-9>)). Decoding attempted handwriting reached 90 characters per minute at 94.1% raw accuracy ([Willett et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03506-2)); speech devices are covered above.
- **Testing the motor pathway.** Magnetic stimulation of human motor cortex through the scalp was first reported in 1985 ([Barker et al., 1985](<https://doi.org/10.1016/s0140-6736(85)92413-4>)). Procedures for clinical and research use are set by an IFCN committee ([Rossini et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinph.2015.02.001)), and paired pulses 1–6 ms apart measure intracortical inhibition ([Kujirai et al., 1993](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.1993.sp019912)).
- **Neurosurgery.** The hand knob lets surgeons find the gyrus on MRI even when anatomy is distorted ([Yousry et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/120.1.141)). Mapping during glioma surgery aims to remove as much tumour as possible while preserving function ([Duffau, 2005](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S1474-4422(05)70140-X>)), and the SCAN-linked bridges across the central sulcus may refine future resection and stimulation ([Skandalakis et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awaf022)).

## Open questions

The core motor role is settled; how the gyrus does it, and how far its cognitive roles reach, are not.

- **Muscles, movements or dynamics?** M1 neurons code both muscle-like and movement-like variables ([Kakei et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.285.5436.2136)), and a dynamical-systems view explains much population activity ([Shenoy et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-neuro-062111-150509)). Critics argue the rotational patterns appear whenever neurons peak in a fixed order, and so reveal little about mechanism ([Lebedev et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-54760-4)).
- **Is M1 needed once a skill is learned?** In rats, motor cortex lesions after training left a learned timing skill intact, but lesions before training prevented learning, so M1 may "tutor" subcortical circuits ([Kawai et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.03.024)). Whether that holds for primate dexterity, which uses direct cortico-motoneuronal pathways ([Rathelot & Strick, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0808362106)), is open.
- **Do mirror responses mean understanding?** Supporters argue the parieto-frontal mirror circuit lets observers grasp others' goals and intentions from the inside ([Rizzolatti & Sinigaglia, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2805)). Critics list eight problems with that theory and argue the human evidence counts against it ([Hickok, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn.2009.21189)).
- **Is word meaning embodied?** Motor-strip activity during reading ([Hauk et al., 2004](<https://doi.org/10.1016/s0896-6273(03)00838-9>)) does not by itself show that meaning depends on it; spreading activation from a disembodied concept fits the same data ([Mahon & Caramazza, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jphysparis.2008.03.004)).
- **How solid is SCAN?** Its anatomy ([Skandalakis et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awaf022)) and a TMS-EEG signature ([Mancuso et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2026.122261)) now have support, but direct causal evidence for what its nodes do is still limited (same source).
- **Does area 4 have a layer 4?** Cajal described one in humans and Brodmann did not; a thin layer IV has now been shown in monkeys ([García-Cabezas & Barbas, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/ejn.12585)), and the human picture is less settled.
- **Why so few receptors?** Area 4 has the smallest summed receptor fingerprint of 44 cortical areas. Myelin could in principle make autoradiography underestimate it, but the authors argue the low values mainly reflect motor cortex's large share of receptor-free myelinated fibres ([Zilles & Palomero-Gallagher, 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnana.2017.00078)).
- **Does imagery use M1?** Imaging meta-analysis finds primary motor cortex is not consistently active during motor imagery, unlike the wider fronto-parietal network ([Hétu et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2013.03.017)).
